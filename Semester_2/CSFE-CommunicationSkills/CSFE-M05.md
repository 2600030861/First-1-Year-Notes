## Presentation Skills (CO5)

*Friend-style notes: intuition first, then the rule, then a worked example.*

## The big picture

A presentation is not "talking for ten minutes". It is a **guided journey** you take an audience on: you say where you are going, you drive them there in small comfortable steps, and at the end you tell them where they have arrived. Everything else — slides,
voice, eye contact, Q&A — is either the road (structure), the vehicle (design), or the driving (delivery).

**The analogy:** an autorickshaw ride in a new city. A good driver says "we'll go via MG Road, it's faster" (roadmap), takes turns that feel smooth (transitions), and says "here we are, that's the market" (close). A bad driver starts the meter and drives in
circles hoping you will work out the route. Most student presentations are the second driver.

**Where you will use it:** lab project reviews, seminar and viva, placement presentations, final-year demo, hackathon pitches, and the "present your findings" part of almost every job. CO5 is also where CO1–CO4 stop being separate subjects — you *listen* to
questions, *speak* the answer, *read* your own slides critically, and *write* the outline first.

**Where the marks are.** In most rubrics, content and structure carry the most weight, delivery second, slide design usually the least. Students over-invest in pretty slides and under-invest in the outline. Do not be that student.

## Basics you must know first

- **Audience** – who is in the room, what they already know, what they will do after. Every design decision depends on this word.
- **Purpose** – the one change you want in the audience's head: "approve our club", "believe our result". One purpose, not four.
- **Central message (thesis)** – one sentence you could say in a lift. If you cannot write it in one line, you have notes, not a presentation.
- **Signposting** – spoken road signs: "first", "that brings me to", "so what does this mean".
- **Visual aid** – a slide, chart, demo or handout that supports your speech. It supports; it does not replace.
- **Speaker notes** – the words you will actually say, kept in the notes pane, not on the slide.
- **Q&A** – the question-and-answer session after (or during) the talk.
- **Rubric** – the marks table your teacher uses. Ask for it. That is not cheating, it is target practice.

## 5.1 Presentation structure and organisation

### Intuition

Your friend calls: "So anyway, the thing is, it depends, but basically I might come tomorrow, actually no, wait." You have no idea what the call was about. Now: "Quick update — I'm coming tomorrow at 10, I'll bring the laptop, and I need your charger." Same
information, but your brain relaxed instantly, because you knew the shape of what was coming.

Audiences are that friend. When they know the shape, they stop spending energy guessing and start spending it listening. **Structure is a kindness, not a formality.**

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Write the one-line message before anything else.** Not "I will talk about our project" but "our attendance app removes proxy attendance in forty seconds of class time". Now every slide can be tested against that sentence: does it support the message
or not?

**Step 2 — Pick exactly three body points.** Three is not magic, but it is the most most listeners hold without notes. Seven things to say means you have a document, not a talk — group them until three remain. *Mini example:* "AI in healthcare" becomes (1)
diagnosis support, (2) hospital operations, (3) the risks; seven facts filed inside three buckets.

**Step 3 — Build the opening in four beats.**

| Beat | Time | Job | Sample line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | 15–20 s | Get attention: a number, a question, a short story | "Last year, 41 of our 60 lab machines sat idle after 6 p.m." |
| Connect | 10 s | Why it matters to *this* room | "That's about 900 student-hours a month we cannot use." |
| Purpose | 10 s | What you will do and what you want | "In ten minutes I'll show three ways to fix that." |
| Roadmap | 15 s | The three points, named | "First the gap, then three options, then what I'm asking for." |

**Step 4 — Follow the "tell them what you'll tell them" rule** — say it three times, in three sizes: *before* (roadmap), *during* (signposts: "that was A, now B"), *after* (summary). This is a **convention of good practice**, often attributed to Dale Carnegie's
advice on speeches, not a law of nature. Where it stops working: very short talks of 2–3 minutes, where the roadmap eats the content, and keynote storytelling, where revealing the ending kills suspense. For college academic and technical talks, always follow it.

**Step 5 — Build the close in three beats:** summary (the three points in one breath) → takeaway (the one sentence you want remembered) → ask (approve, fund, try, ask questions). Then a plain "Thank you — happy to take questions." Never end on "so, yeah, that's
it."

**Step 6 — Budget time before you write slides.** For ten minutes: opening 1.0 min, body 6.5 min, close 1.5 min, buffer 1.0 min. At a comfortable presentation pace of **130–150 words per minute** that is roughly 1,300–1,500 words of speech. Conversation runs
faster (160–190 wpm); presenting is slower because you pause. Reading a script at conversation speed is the number-one reason students overrun by three minutes.

**Step 7 — Turn minutes into slides:** one slide per 1–2 minutes of speaking, so 7–10 content slides plus a title for a ten-minute talk. Twenty-two slides means you exported a report, not designed a talk.

### The sample outline (memorise this shape)

```text
TITLE SLIDE        project title / your name / guide / date
1. HOOK            0:00-0:20   one number or one question, big type
2. WHY IT MATTERS  0:20-1:00   purpose + three-word roadmap on one slide
3. POINT 1         1:00-3:15   claim -> evidence -> so what
4. POINT 2         3:15-5:30   claim -> evidence -> so what
5. POINT 3         5:30-7:30   claim -> evidence -> so what
6. WHAT IT MEANS   7:30-8:30   three points tied into one idea
7. THE ASK         8:30-9:20   what you want the audience to do
8. THANK YOU/Q&A   9:20-10:00  name, contact, "happy to take questions"
```

Every body slide has the same inner shape: **claim → evidence → so what**. Claim is what you want them to believe; evidence is a number, a demo or a comparison; "so what" connects it back to the audience. Claim with no evidence is an assertion; evidence with no
"so what" makes the audience do your thinking.

**Example question:** You have **10 minutes** before the principal and five faculty members to argue that your college needs a coding club. Write the full outline with time budget, slide count, and the exact words of your opening and closing.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Fix the message:* "One hour a week, one lab, two faculty mentors — a coding club makes placement-relevant practice routine instead of optional."

*Step 2 — Three body points:* (1) the gap — what students do not currently get; (2) the proposal — what the club concretely is; (3) the proof — that it works elsewhere and costs almost nothing.

*Step 3 — Budget:* opening 1.0 min (2 slides) · gap 2.0 min (2) · proposal 2.5 min (2) · proof 2.0 min (2) · close 1.5 min (1) · buffer 1.0 min = **10.0 min, 9 slides**.

*Step 4 — Opening, verbatim (≈140 words ≈ 60 s):*

> "Sir, ma'am — one number. In our last two placement drives, 38% of shortlisted students cleared the coding round. The rest cleared the interview. They did not lack communication; they lacked practice at solving problems under time. That gap is fixable, and it
> needs no new building, no new lab, no new budget line. In the next ten minutes I'll make three points: what the gap actually is; exactly what a coding club would look like — day, time, room, mentors; and evidence that it works, at close to zero cost. At the
> end I'll ask for one hour, one lab, two mentors."

*Step 5 — Closing, verbatim (≈200 words ≈ 85 s):*

> "To bring it together. First the gap: a third of our shortlisted students lose offers at the coding round, and that is a practice problem, not a talent problem. Second the proposal: one hour a week, Saturday 4 to 5, Lab 3, run by two final-year students with
> two faculty mentors, on a simple ladder — basics, contests, interview sets. Third the proof: this already runs in colleges our size, and our own students have asked for it — 96 signatures in three days, which I have here. If you remember one sentence, let it
> be this: the cheapest placement improvement available to us is an hour a week we already own. I'm asking for three things: Lab 3 on Saturdays from 4 to 5, two faculty mentors, and a notice on the department board. Nothing else. Thank you — happy to take
> questions."

*Step 6 — Check:* 140 + 200 words ≈ 2.4 min of the 2.5 minutes budgeted for opening and close; the body gets 6.5 min ≈ 900 words, about 300 per point; nine slides. Fits with a minute spare.

**Why this works:** the hook is a number with a consequence, not "today I will talk about"; the roadmap names the points *and* previews the ask, so nobody wonders what you want; each point carries evidence rather than assertion; the ask is small and specific
(small asks get approved, "support our club" gets a nod and nothing else); and the close repeats all three points so someone who dozed off at minute four still leaves with the message.

⚠️ **Common trap:** students plan the body in detail and improvise the opening and close, then overrun and end with "so that's basically it". The close is where the decision and the marks live — **script the first and last 60 seconds word for word** and rehearse
only those; the middle survives on keyword cards.

## 5.2 Effective slide and visual design

### Intuition

A slide is a **billboard**, not a page. You get about three seconds before someone decides whether to read it or ignore it — exactly like a hoarding on a highway, and nobody reads a billboard with eleven lines of 12-point text while driving. The test: if the
slide were printed and stuck on the far wall, could you read the main line from here? If not, it is a document pretending to be a slide. Documents are fine; they belong in a handout, not on a projector.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — One idea per slide.** Not one *topic*, one *idea*. If the title needs an "and" ("Results and Limitations"), it is two slides. *Mini example:* "Method and Results" becomes "How we trained the model" and "What accuracy we got".

**Step 2 — Use the 6x6 rule as a starting point:** roughly 6 bullet lines of roughly 6 words each. This is a widely used heuristic, not a standard — a guardrail against walls of text. A results table, a code snippet or a system diagram may break it completely
and should. The absolute rule: **if the audience is reading your slide, they are not listening to you.**

**Step 3 — Get the type sizes right.** Title 32–40 pt; body 22–28 pt; captions and sources 16–18 pt; never below 14 pt. One or two fonts only, and a clean sans-serif (Calibri, Arial, Segoe UI, Helvetica) projects better at distance than a serif. Bold the one
word you want landing, not whole paragraphs.

**Step 4 — Control contrast and colour.** Pick dark-on-light or light-on-dark and stay consistent. Never put text on a photograph without a solid or semi-transparent block behind it. Avoid red-on-green pairs — red-green colour blindness affects roughly 1 in 12
men and 1 in 200 women — and never let colour be the *only* signal; pair it with a label, shape or pattern. Three colours is plenty: dark for text, light for background, one accent. The 60-30-10 split (mostly background, some secondary, a little accent) is a
handy convention, not a rule.

**Step 5 — Choose the chart that matches the question in the audience's head.**

| Question | Use | Not this |
|---|---|---|
| "How did it change over time?" | Line chart | Pie chart |
| "How do these categories compare?" | Bar chart (horizontal for long labels) | Line chart |
| "What is the split of the whole?" | Pie or stacked bar, **max ~5 slices** | Pie with 11 slices |
| "Are these two things related?" | Scatter plot | Bar chart |
| "What is the exact value?" | Table | Any 3-D chart |
| "Where does the time go?" | Gantt or timeline | Pie chart |

Two more rules that buy marks: **label axes with units** ("Accuracy (%), n = 12,000 images") and **delete 3-D, shadows, gradient fills and gridline clutter**. Every decoration is a chance to look at the wrong thing.

**Step 6 — Cut ruthlessly.** Delete full sentences (keep keywords; you will say the sentence), the department logo on all nine slides (title slide is enough), animations and fly-ins (they cost rehearsal time and break flow), clip art and stock photos of
handshakes, and any slide you would have to apologise for ("this one is a bit small, sorry").

**Step 7 — Before/after critique.** A real student slide:

```text
BEFORE  Slide 4: Introduction
- Our project is basically about developing a web based application which uses
  machine learning algorithms, specifically a convolutional neural network, to
  detect plant diseases from images uploaded by farmers, and this is very
  important because agriculture is the backbone of India and farmers lose a lot
  of money every year due to plant diseases, so we thought of doing this project
  and we have also compared three models and the results are given later.
```

What is wrong, in order of damage: one slide carrying four ideas; 88 words at 12 pt, unreadable past row three, so the audience gives up; written to be *spoken* ("basically", "so we thought of doing"); a promise instead of content ("results are given later");
and not one number, so nothing is memorable.

```text
AFTER   Slide 4: The problem in one line
BIG     A farmer waits 3-7 days for a disease diagnosis.
SMALL   Our app: one photo -> answer in 5 seconds.
FOOTER  Test set: 12,000 labelled leaf images (source: our dataset sheet)
```

**Why the after works:** one idea; eleven words of main text; a before/after contrast the audience can *feel*; a footer that quietly proves real work; and room for you to say the sentences out loud, where they belong. Any figure you use must come from your own
data sheet — never invent a number to make a slide look sharper.

### The slide checklist (run before every submission)

- Title is a claim, not a label ("Accuracy fell after 40 epochs", not "Results").
- One idea, sayable in one line.
- Nothing below 18 pt in the body.
- Six bullets or fewer, six words or fewer — or a justified exception (table, diagram, code).
- Every chart: axis labels, units, and the source of the data.
- Contrast checked from the back of the room, not from your chair.
- No animation except one that reveals a comparison you are about to explain.

**Example question:** Rewrite this slide for a three-minute project update, and say which chart replaces the accuracy sentence: *"Results — We tested our model on three datasets and got accuracy of 94% on dataset A which had 8000 images, 89% on dataset B which
had 5000 images and 91% on dataset C which had 3000 images, and also the training time was 40 minutes for A, 25 for B and 15 for C, and we also noticed that accuracy drops when the images are blurry which is a limitation."*

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Count the ideas:* two — accuracy across datasets, and the blurry-image limitation. So two slides, not one.

*Step 2 — Slide A:* title "Accuracy holds above 89% across all three datasets"; a **grouped bar chart** (x = dataset A, B, C; y = accuracy %) with data labels 94 / 89 / 91; footer "Images: 8,000 / 5,000 / 3,000. Training: 40 / 25 / 15 min."

*Step 3 — Slide B:* title "Blur is where the model breaks"; big line "Sharp images 94% → blurred images 71%"; small line "Next step: blur augmentation in training". *(Use your own measured figure for the blurred case.)*

*Step 4 — Justify the chart:* the audience's question is "how do the three datasets compare?" — comparison across categories → bar chart. A line chart would falsely imply the datasets sit on a continuum; a pie chart is meaningless because accuracies are not
parts of a whole.

*Step 5 — Decide what to cut:* training times become a footer, not a third chart, because they are supporting detail. "We noticed accuracy drops when images are blurry" becomes a claim with a number, because without one it is an impression, not a result.

**Why this works:** every number survives, so nothing is lost for the examiner, but the audience gets two clear claims instead of one ninety-word sentence. Cutting information from the *slide* is not cutting it from the *presentation* — you say the rest.

⚠️ **Common trap:** reading the slide aloud. If the slide has the sentence and your mouth has the same sentence, the audience reads ahead, finishes before you and disengages. The mirror trap is 8-point text "so that everything is included" — that slide scores as
*no slide at all*, because nobody can read it.

## 5.3 Verbal and non-verbal delivery

### Intuition

Two people cook the same biryani from the same recipe; one is memorable, one is not. The recipe was identical — the *cooking* differed. Content is the recipe, delivery is the cooking. And delivery is not "being a natural": it is six adjustable knobs — **pace,
pause, volume, eye contact, gesture, posture** — each of which improves in a week if you record yourself once and change one knob at a time.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Pace: aim for 130–150 words per minute.** Nervous speakers speed up, sometimes to 200 wpm, which is why they deliver ten minutes of content in six. The rule: **slow down for numbers and for the sentence after a pause; speed up through familiar
transitions.** *Mini example:* "Our accuracy is ninety-four per cent", said about 20% slower than the paragraph around it, is the only line people will remember.

**Step 2 — Pause: silence is punctuation.**

| Pause | Length | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Micro-pause | 0.5 s | Instead of "um" |
| Power pause | 2–3 s | Right after your key claim |
| Transition pause | 1–2 s | Between body points, while you move |

The power pause feels like ten seconds to you and like confidence to them. It is also the cheapest cure for filler words: your mouth fills silence with "um", so give it a planned silence instead.

**Step 3 — Volume and projection.** Speak to the last row, not the first. Projection comes from breath support, not shouting: stand tall, breathe from the belly, aim your voice at a point on the back wall. With a microphone, keep it a hand-span from your mouth
and do not turn your head away mid-sentence.

**Step 4 — Eye contact: three anchors.** Do not try to look at "everyone". Pick three anchor people — left, centre, right — and hold each for 3–5 seconds per sentence-group, sweeping slowly between them; everyone in that zone feels included. On a video call,
look at the **lens**, not the faces on screen.

**Step 5 — Gesture: inside the box.** Keep hands in an imaginary box from waist to shoulders, in front of you. Open palms read as honest, palms down as calming, counting on fingers as structure ("three reasons — one…"). One gesture per idea is enough. Hands in
pockets, crossed arms or a fiddled pen all read as nervousness, and pen-fiddling is worse than no gesture because it is visible rhythm noise.

**Step 6 — Posture.** Feet about shoulder-width, weight even, shoulders back and down, chin level. Stand still for a key point — walking while talking splits attention. Move deliberately between points, then stop and plant.

**Step 7 — Filler words.** The usual suspects: *um, uh, like, basically, actually, you know, I mean, so yeah*. They are not a character flaw; they are what your mouth does while your brain searches. Three fixes, best first: (1) **replace with a pause** —
instant, free; (2) **slow the sentence** before the hard word; (3) **plan your transitions**, because fillers cluster exactly where you improvise a link. *Mini example:* "So, basically, we, uh, used a CNN because, like, it works well on images" → "We used a CNN.
Why? Because it is built for images." Shorter *and* stronger.

**Step 8 — Handling nerves.** Nerves are arousal, and arousal is fuel. **Reframe**: "I'm excited" beats "I'm calm" as a self-instruction, because both are arousal and one is labelled useful. **Breathe 4-4-6**: in for 4, hold 4, out for 6, five times, two minutes
before you start — the long exhale slows the heart rate. **Arrive early and walk the room** so it stops being strange. **Memorise only the first and last 60 seconds**; everything between lives on keyword cards, because memorising the whole script is the fastest
route to a blank mind — one forgotten word takes the whole paragraph with it. **Use a physical anchor**: feet planted, hands on the lectern for two seconds, one breath, begin.

### The delivery checklist (five minutes before you speak)

- First and last 60 seconds memorised; nothing else.
- Keyword cards only — maximum eight, one idea each, held in one hand.
- Water within reach; sip before the first sentence, not during it.
- Phone silent and off the lectern; clicker tested; standing position chosen.
- Two power pauses marked on the cards with a slash.
- Three eye-contact anchors chosen in the room.
- One sentence decided in advance for "I don't know" (see 5.5).

**Example question:** Diagnose the delivery of this opening and rewrite it with delivery notes: *"So, um, hi everyone, my name is Rahul and, uh, today I'm going to basically talk about our project which is, like, a face-recognition attendance system, so, yeah,
attendance is a big problem in colleges because, um, teachers waste a lot of time, and, actually, we thought, you know, that we could solve this with AI, so, uh, let's see."*

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Count the damage.* Eleven fillers in 45 seconds (*um* ×3, *uh* ×3, *like*, *basically*, *actually*, *you know*, *so yeah*). No hook — it opens with name plus "I'm going to talk about", the least interesting possible start. One run-on sentence with six
commas and no full stop. It ends on "let's see", which signals doubt about your own content. Pace: 66 words in 45 s ≈ 88 wpm — slow *because* of the fillers, not deliberately, so the problem is not speed but **absence of pacing**.

*Step 2 — Rewrite (≈65 words ≈ 28 s at 140 wpm):*

> "Hi, I'm Rahul. [pause 1 s] In most of our classes, the first four minutes go to calling out roll numbers. [power pause 2 s] Ours takes forty seconds — one camera, no signatures, no proxies. [pause] I'll show you how it works, what it gets wrong, and what it
> would cost to run in one department."

*Step 3 — Delivery notes for the card:*

```text
[stand still, feet planted]   "Hi, I'm Rahul."
[PAUSE 1s]
[slow, deliberate]            "the first four minutes go to roll numbers"
[POWER PAUSE 2s - look at anchor L]
[normal pace]                 "forty seconds - one camera, no proxies"
[pause, then count on fingers] how it works / what it gets wrong / cost
```

*Step 4 — The three knobs to change first:* replace every filler with a pause; open with the problem, not your name and agenda; end the segment on a promise ("I'll show you three things") instead of "let's see".

**Why this works:** the rewrite is 65 words against 66, so it is not shorter — it is *denser*. It swaps nine hedges for two designed pauses, which reads as control, and its last line gives the audience a map, which is 5.1 doing its job in the first thirty
seconds.

⚠️ **Common trap:** practising by reading the script silently. Silent reading trains nothing — you cannot hear your own fillers or pace. **Record once on your phone and listen at 1.25x speed**: fillers become unmissable, and one recorded run fixes what three
silent readings cannot.

## 5.4 Communicating technical content to diverse audiences

### Intuition

You already do this daily: explaining UPI to your grandmother, to a classmate, and to a bank clerk are three performances of the same fact. You do not change the truth; you change the **distance you start from**. The failure mode is the **curse of knowledge** —
once you understand something you cannot remember not understanding it, so you say "we used a CNN with dropout and augmentation" and genuinely believe that was clear. To a non-specialist it was three unknown words in a row.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Do audience analysis on four questions.**

| Question | What it changes |
|---|---|
| Who are they? (role, background) | Vocabulary, and how much background you give |
| What do they already know? | What you may skip without apology |
| What do they need from you? | Which of your five findings get airtime |
| What will they do afterwards? | Your ask and your close |

*Mini example:* the same project in three rooms. **Guide's review** wants method, validation, limitations. **Placement panel** wants what you built, what you learned, what you can do on day one. **School outreach** wants "computers can help farmers". Same
content, three decks.

**Step 2 — Build the analogy ladder.** Write your project at three rungs and keep all three in your notes; you will use rung 3 in a viva, rung 2 with other branches, rung 1 at a family function or outreach talk.

| Rung | Audience | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — General | No CS background | No jargon. One everyday analogy. Only intuitive numbers. |
| 2 — Peer | Same course, different project | Project-level jargon allowed if defined once. Compare with things they know. |
| 3 — Expert | Guide, examiner, senior engineer | Full terminology, but state assumptions, baselines and limitations explicitly. |

**Step 3 — Handle jargon with three moves, in order.** **Replace** if a plain word exists ("we cached results" → "we saved the answer so we don't recompute it"). **Define on first use** in one clause ("a CNN, a type of model that learns patterns in images, …")
and move on — do not stop to teach. **Park it** if it needs real explanation: "I'll use the term *inference*; it just means running the model on a new photo — I'll come back to it if anyone wants the detail."

**Step 4 — Run three tests.** The **grandmother test**: would a smart adult outside your field follow the first 60 seconds? The **"so what" test**: after each technical sentence, can you add "which means…" in plain words? If not, the sentence is decoration. The
**acronym audit**: more than about five acronyms in a short talk means you are speaking to yourself — never assume API, GPU, CNN, LSTM or CRUD are free.

**Step 5 — The full worked example: your project at three levels.** Project: a face-recognition attendance system.

*Rung 1 — general (≈50 words):* "In most classes the teacher spends the first few minutes calling roll numbers, and students answer for absent friends. Our system is a camera at the door: it looks at each face as you walk in, like a security guard who never
forgets a face, and marks you present. Forty seconds for the whole class." *Where the analogy stops working:* a guard can be fooled by a photo held up to the camera — which is exactly the limitation you mention next.

*Rung 2 — peer (≈110 words):* "A two-stage pipeline. Stage one is detection: a detector returns a bounding box per face in the frame. Stage two is recognition: each box becomes a 128-number signature — an embedding — which we compare against stored signatures
for that class by distance, with a threshold. Closest match under the threshold is marked present; anything above goes into an 'unrecognised' bucket for the teacher. The interesting part is the threshold: too loose and proxies get in, too tight and real students
get rejected for lighting. We tuned it on our own data and got 94% correct on 12,000 test images."

*Rung 3 — expert (≈160 words):* "RetinaFace-style detection, then a FaceNet-style embedding with triplet loss, fine-tuned on our department's 210-student set. Each identity is stored as a 128-D embedding; matching is cosine distance at a threshold of 0.42,
chosen on a held-out validation split by maximising F1 for the 'present' class. Baselines: raw pixel k-NN 71%, pre-trained without fine-tuning 86%, ours 94% — so fine-tuning contributes about eight points. Failure analysis: 62% of errors are low-light frames,
21% partially occluded faces. Our limitation is anti-spoofing: a printed photo can pass, so we do not claim liveness detection. The test split is by identity, not by image, to avoid leakage."

Notice what changed across the rungs: the *claims* stayed identical. Only vocabulary, evidence density, and how explicitly limitations are stated changed. Rung 3 is the only one naming baselines and leakage — that is what an expert audience buys you: credit for
precision.

**Example question:** Your project is an automatic MCQ answer-sheet evaluator. In **2 minutes**, explain it to a panel with one CS professor, one mathematics professor and one non-technical placement officer, so that all three stay with you. Write the script and
mark where you switch levels.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Analysis:* three audiences in one room → one **spine at rung 1**, two short **rung-3 asides** for the CS professor, and one *accuracy* number for the maths professor. The placement officer needs the outcome, not the pipeline.

*Step 2 — Budget for 2 min (≈280 words):* rung-1 spine 120 words, two asides 60 each, close 40.

*Step 3 — Script:*

> **[RUNG 1 — all three]** "Every semester our department evaluates roughly 6,000 MCQ sheets by hand. Each has 50 bubbles. Two people count, and they still disagree on about 2% of sheets — usually a faint pencil mark or a half-filled bubble. [*pause*] Our system
> photographs a stack of sheets, reads every bubble, and gives a score sheet in under a minute for a class of 60. Think of a scanner that knows the difference between 'filled' and 'almost filled' — that difference is exactly what humans argue about."
> **[RUNG 2 — for the CS professor]** "Technically: we deskew the image, detect four corner fiducials to fix rotation, then sample mean intensity inside each bubble region and threshold it — adaptively per sheet, not globally, because photocopier contrast
> varies."
> **[RUNG 3 — the number for the maths professor]** "On 500 sheets checked by hand we agreed with the human score on 496; all four disagreements were genuinely ambiguous half-filled bubbles. Mean absolute error: 0.4 marks out of 50."
> **[RUNG 3 — one limitation, stated before it is asked]** "It cannot read handwriting, so descriptive answers are out of scope. And it needs the four corner marks printed — a sheet with a torn corner is rejected rather than guessed at."
> **[CLOSE — rung 1]** "So: sixty sheets a minute, agreement with human marking on 99% of sheets, and it tells you which sheets it is unsure about instead of guessing. Thank you."

*Step 4 — Mark the switches:* four in two minutes is right for a mixed room. More and the non-specialists lose the thread; fewer and the CS professor concludes there is nothing technical inside.

**Why this works:** the non-technical listener gets a spine they never lose (counting sheets, saving time, settling arguments); the technical listeners get two precise asides and one honest limitation, which is what earns technical credibility; and the "unsure"
behaviour is described in plain language *and* is a real design decision. Nobody is talked down to — the maths professor gets the most rigorous number in the talk.

⚠️ **Common trap:** confusing **simplifying** with **dumbing down**. Simplifying removes vocabulary; dumbing down removes the evidence. "It's AI, it just works" is dumbing down and is penalised heavily. Also: when you use an analogy, say where it breaks — "like
a security guard, except a guard can be fooled by a photograph, and so can we" earns more than the analogy alone.

## 5.5 Handling questions

### Intuition

Q&A is not a test you can fail; it is the part where the audience tells you what they actually heard. Two presenters can give the same talk and the calmer one scores higher, because calm answering is *evidence* that the work is yours. Everyone has gaps — what is
marked is not omniscience, it is **how you behave when you don't know**.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Use the pause-repeat-answer-check loop (PRAC).** Four moves, about fifteen seconds.

| Move | Time | What you do | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P**ause | 2 s | Stop, breathe, do not start talking immediately | Stops you answering the wrong question at speed |
| **R**epeat | 5 s | "So you're asking whether the threshold was tuned per class" | Everyone hears it; you confirm you understood |
| **A**nswer | 20–40 s | Direct answer first, then one reason or example | Answer-first is what a listener needs |
| **C**heck | 3 s | "Does that cover it?" | Catches the real question hiding behind the asked one |

*Mini example:* Q: "Why didn't you use a transformer?" A: "We did try one. [*pause*] You're asking why we didn't ship it — [*repeat*] it was three points better but six times slower on CPU, so it lost on the constraint that mattered: a lab machine with no GPU.
[*check*] Does that answer it, or did you mean the architecture choice?"

**Step 2 — Answer claim-first.** Weak: "Well, we thought about it, and there were several considerations, and eventually, after testing, we decided…" — the listener waits twenty seconds for the answer. Strong: "No. The CPU budget ruled it out. Here's the detail
if you want it."

**Step 3 — When you don't know, use one of four honest bridges.** Never invent a number.

| Situation | Say this |
|---|---|
| Data you don't have | "I don't have that number with me. I can send it by tomorrow — may I note your email?" |
| Outside your scope | "That's outside what we tested. My guess would be X, but I'd want to verify before I claim it." |
| Needs a long answer | "That deserves more than thirty seconds. Can I take it after the session, or send a short write-up?" |
| You genuinely erred | "You're right, that's an error on the slide. The correct figure is Y. Thank you." |

**Step 4 — Handle hostile questions by separating person from point.** A hostile question is usually a legitimate question in a bad tone. Do not mirror the tone; keep your volume and pace exactly where they were. Acknowledge the legitimate part ("that's a fair
challenge — if the baseline were unfair, the whole result falls"), answer only that part, and time-box: one answer, not three rounds. **Never try to win** — being right and sounding aggressive loses marks.

**Step 5 — Handle confused questions by re-framing.** The question is often not the question. Ask a **closed clarifying question** — "Do you mean the training data or the test data?" — then answer the simpler version first, at rung 1 if needed, and offer the
detail afterwards.

**Step 6 — Split multi-part questions.** "Three things were asked: cost, timeline, privacy. Cost first — …" Splitting is itself a mark-earning move, because it shows you were listening.

**Step 7 — Manage the room.** Repeat every question so the back row hears it. If five hands go up, "let's take three, then I'll be around afterwards." Keep a **parking lot** — a corner of the board or a notes page for questions you will answer later — and say so
out loud.

### Three sample exchanges

> **1 — hostile.** Q: "This is basically the same as the 2019 paper. What's actually new here?" A: [*pause*] "You're asking what's different from the 2019 work. Two things: that paper assumed a fixed threshold, and we tune it per class, which is where our
> eight-point gain comes from; and we report failure cases by lighting condition, which they don't. [*check*] If you meant the architecture, then yes — that part is the same, and I should have said so on slide 6."

> **2 — "I don't know".** Q: "What's the latency on a Raspberry Pi 4?" A: [*pause*] "On a Pi 4 specifically — I don't have that measurement. We ran on a laptop CPU at 210 milliseconds per frame. My expectation is the Pi would be several times slower, but I won't
> quote a number I haven't measured. I can run that test and mail you the result this week."

> **3 — confused.** Q: "So does it work offline or what?" A: [*pause*] "Let me answer the right thing — are you asking whether the app needs internet during class, or whether the model can be trained without it?" Q: "During class." A: "Then yes, fully offline.
> The model runs on the phone; only the final attendance sheet syncs when there's network."

**Example question:** Answer both questions using the techniques above and explain what each answer is doing. (a) "Your accuracy is 94% — but on how many students? That sounds like a toy dataset," asked sharply. (b) "How does this compare to the commercial
system the college already pays for?" — you have never seen that system's internals.

**Solution:**

*(a)* Pause, do not react to the tone; repeat the factual core:

> "You're asking how big the evaluation set is — whether 94% is meaningful. [*pause*] It's 210 students, 12,000 images, split by identity so no student appears in both train and test. You're right that 210 is small: one department, one semester. So I'd call 94%
> an internal number, not a general one — the honest claim is '94% on our own cohort', which is what the slide says. Generalising needs more identities, not more images."

*What it is doing:* concedes the true part (small dataset), refuses the false part (nothing was hidden), and upgrades the questioner's point into a limitation you already own. Tone is answered with calm, not defence; the marks come from the concession.

*(b)* Repeat, then split:

> "You're asking how we compare to the system the college already runs. [*pause*] I can answer half of that honestly: I haven't seen their internals, so I can't compare architectures. What I measured is that our pipeline runs offline on a phone, and theirs, as
> far as I know from using it, needs the office network. On accuracy I can't make a claim, because I haven't tested theirs. If it's useful, I can ask the office for a demo sheet, run both on the same 500 images, and send you the comparison."

*What it is doing:* draws a clean line between **what I measured** and **what I don't know**, turns the gap into a deliverable, and refuses to invent a competitor's numbers. That behaviour is what separates a good viva from an excellent one.

**Why these work:** both start with the answer, not a story; both concede something real within two sentences, which lowers the temperature immediately; neither invents a figure; and both end with a check or an offer, so the exchange does not spiral.

⚠️ **Common trap:** three of them. Starting every answer with "That's a very good question!" — audible wallpaper that eats your thirty seconds. Answering a different, easier question out of nerves — examiners notice and ask again, harder. And arguing to win: you
are not in a debate, you are being marked on composure, so "you're right, I'll fix that slide" scores higher than defending it.

## 5.6 Team and academic presentations

### Intuition

A team presentation is a relay race, not four solo races back to back. In a relay the baton handover is practised — that is where races are won and lost — and in a team presentation the handover is the **transition line**, the one part almost every team forgets
to rehearse. Audiences judge teams on two things that cannot be faked: does the story have one spine, and does everyone know what the others said.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Split by content, not by slide count.** Bad: "you take slides 1–5, I take 6–10" — that splits mid-idea. Good: "you take the problem and the method, I take the results and the implications." Each person owns a **complete thought** with a beginning and
an end.

**Step 2 — Give everyone equal speaking time.** For 12 minutes with four speakers: three minutes each. A rough convention that works: whoever owns the problem speaks the introduction, whoever owns the conclusion speaks the close, and the "ask" is delivered by
the team lead. It is a convention, not a rule — what matters is that airtime is visibly balanced, because unbalanced airtime reads as unbalanced contribution and is often marked that way.

**Step 3 — Rehearse the handover as a unit — the handoff sandwich.**

| Beat | Who | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Land the last point | Speaker 1 | "So the gap is a practice gap, not a talent gap." |
| Introduce the next | Speaker 1 | "Which raises what we'd actually run. Aditi will take that." |
| Accept and confirm | Speaker 2 | "Thanks, Rahul. Yes — the design. Three decisions mattered." |

Three sentences, six seconds, no "over to you" mumbling and no awkward clicker fumbling. Rehearse all of them; they are the cheapest marks in the deck.

**Step 4 — One deck, one template, one style.** One person owns the file; everyone uses the same slide master, fonts and accent colour. Nothing looks more like four separate projects than four different title styles.

**Step 5 — Assign the Q&A map in advance.** Write down who answers which area: data questions → whoever built the dataset; method → the modeller; cost, timeline, next steps → the lead. If a question lands elsewhere, the wrong person must not answer it — "That's
Meera's area — Meera?" is a *strength*, not an evasion.

**Step 6 — Academic presentations: seminar and viva.**

```text
SEMINAR (15-20 min + Q&A)
  title, context, why this topic       1.5 min
  background / what is known           2.5 min
  gap / research question              1.5 min
  method (reproducible)                4.0 min
  results (one chart per claim)        4.0 min
  discussion: limitations              2.5 min
  conclusion + future work             1.5 min
  references slide (never read aloud)    -
```

A **viva** follows different rules: examiners **interrupt**, which is normal, not hostile. Expect "why this method", "what would break it", "what did *you* personally do", "show me that number again". Keep answers to thirty seconds unless invited to expand, and
know your own contribution precisely — "I built the detector and the evaluation harness; my teammate built the app." Vague ownership is the commonest viva failure.

**Step 7 — The coordination checklist.** One master deck file with a named owner and two backups · one shared template, identical fonts and colours · a time budget per speaker, everyone rehearsed to the clock · all transition lines scripted and rehearsed aloud ·
Q&A map written down · two full team run-throughs, one in front of two live humans · files on a pen drive *and* emailed to yourself · one person owning the clicker and the one-minute warning signal · arrive twenty minutes early and test projector, audio, aspect
ratio and fonts · agree dress code, water, and where each person stands.

**Example question:** Your four-member team has **12 minutes** to present "AI in healthcare" to a mixed panel of doctors and faculty. Give (i) the content split with timings, (ii) the exact transition lines between speakers 1 and 2, and (iii) the Q&A map.

**Solution:**

*(i) Split — one spine, four complete thoughts:* A (lead) — hook, why healthcare, roadmap, then closing summary and the ask, 3.0 min, slides 1–3 and 9 · B — diagnosis support: imaging, what the models actually do, 3.0 min, slides 4–5 · C — operations:
scheduling, triage, documentation, 3.0 min, slides 6–7 · D — the risks: bias, privacy, accountability, honest limits, 2.5 min, slide 8 · all — buffer 0.5 min. Total 12.0. Note A gets the bookends but no extra content: that is how you keep airtime equal while
giving the story one voice.

*(ii) Transition lines:*

> **A → B:** "So the opportunity is real, but every one of these three areas has failed somewhere. The one with the most evidence behind it is diagnosis. Bhavya will show you what the models actually do — and, importantly, what they don't."
> **B:** "Thanks, Aarav. Yes — three things matter: what the model sees, what it was trained on, and what happens when it's wrong. Starting with what it sees…"

*(iii) Q&A map:* datasets, accuracy, validation → B · hospital workflow, cost, staffing → C · ethics, privacy, liability, bias → D · overall scope, next steps, collaboration → A. Standard out-of-turn line: "That's Bhavya's area — Bhavya?"

**Why this works:** each speaker owns a complete argument, so no handover lands mid-thought; the transition is a *content bridge* ("the opportunity is real, but… the most evidence is in diagnosis") rather than a stage direction, so the audience never notices the
seam; and the Q&A map means nobody is caught bluffing in someone else's area, which is the fastest way for a team to lose credibility as a unit.

⚠️ **Common trap:** the empty handover — "…and now my teammate will continue." Six seconds of dead air, attention drops, and the next speaker starts cold. The other one: a single member speaking 60% of the time. Even if their content is best, the panel marks
*teamwork*, and they can see the clock.

## 5.7 Rehearsal, feedback and self/peer assessment

### Intuition

Nobody is born able to present, and nobody improves by presenting. People improve by **rehearsing with feedback** — attempt, observe, change one thing, attempt again — the same loop you use for a musical instrument or a sport. A presentation you never rehearsed
is a first attempt being marked. The discipline that matters: **one change per rehearsal**, because change five things and you cannot tell which one helped.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Follow a schedule, not "I'll practise at home".**

| When | What | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| T–7 days | Content lock: outline fixed, slides drafted (2 h) | Structure only |
| T–5 days | Solo run, timed, standing, out loud | Does it fit the clock? |
| T–4 days | Record on phone, watch once at 1.25x (20 min) | Fillers, pace, eye contact |
| T–3 days | Fix the one worst thing, run again | One change only |
| T–2 days | Full run in front of two people, with Q&A | Questions, handovers |
| T–1 day | Tech check: projector, fonts, video, clicker | Nothing new |
| Day | Warm-up: first and last 60 s aloud, plus breathing | Calm |

Minimum viable version: **three full timed runs**, one recorded, one in front of a human. Fewer than three and you will overrun.

**Step 2 — Rehearse in three passes, each with a different job.** **Content pass** (sitting, no clock): is the argument right? Cut any point that does not serve the message. **Timing pass** (standing, clock, no slides): does it fit? If you overrun by two
minutes, cut content — never speak faster. **Delivery pass** (standing, slides, recorded): fillers, pauses, eye contact, handovers.

**Step 3 — Use a feedback rubric with marks.** A workable 100-mark rubric for a ten-minute individual talk:

| Criterion | Marks | 1 (weak) | 3 (adequate) | 5 (strong) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Content & structure | 30 | No clear message; random order; no close | Message present; three points; thin close | Clear thesis; three signposted points; opening and close scripted and tight |
| Visual design | 20 | Text walls; <14 pt; unlabelled charts | Readable; some clutter; charts mostly right | One idea per slide; chart matched to question; every number sourced |
| Delivery | 25 | Monotone, rushed, reads slides, no eye contact | Audible, some pauses, occasional eye contact | 130–150 wpm; power pauses; three anchors; purposeful gestures |
| Engagement & Q&A | 15 | Ignores audience; off-target or bluffs | Some repetition; mostly on target | PRAC loop; concedes limits; never invents a number |
| Teamwork (teams only) | 10 | Unequal airtime; empty handovers | Balanced; functional handovers | Scripted content bridges; Q&A map followed |

Score each criterion 1–5 and multiply by its weight ÷ 5. Example: content 4 → 24/30, design 3 → 12/20, delivery 4 → 20/25, Q&A 3 → 9/15, teamwork 4 → 8/10; total **73**.

**Step 4 — Ask for feedback that can be acted on.** "How was it?" earns "it was good". Ask instead: "Where exactly did you lose track of me?" · "Which single slide would you cut?" · "Did I answer the question I was asked, or a different one?" · "Was the pace too
fast at any point — which minute?"

**Step 5 — Convert vague comments into actions** using *observation → specific moment → one change*.

| Vague | Actionable |
|---|---|
| "You seemed nervous." | "Your hands were in your pockets from 2:10 to 3:40. Next run: hands in the box, one gesture per point." |
| "Slides are too busy." | "Slide 6 has 14 bullets at 16 pt. Cut to six; move the rest to speaker notes." |
| "Good energy but rushed." | "You finished 2:40 early, so pace was about 175 wpm. Next run: three power pauses, and slow the numbers." |

**Step 6 — Self-assessment, done properly:** watch the recording three times, once per channel. **Muted** — body language only: posture, hands, movement, eye direction. **Audio only, screen off** — fillers, pace, pauses, volume; this changes behaviour fastest.
**Transcript or notes** — structure: did you signpost, and did the claim come before the evidence?

**Step 7 — Peer assessment, done fairly:** score anonymously against the same rubric, then discuss. Two rules — comment on **behaviour, not the person** ("the middle section had three claims on one slide", not "your slides were bad"), and give **one strength
plus one change**; more than one change per round is noise.

**Example question:** Your friend presents for ten minutes. You note: she finished in 7:20; she read most slides aloud; her three body points were good; she said "basically" 14 times; her hands were in her pockets; in Q&A, asked about dataset size, she talked
about model architecture. Fill the rubric (1–5 per criterion), compute the total out of 100, and write the three most useful pieces of feedback.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Score against the descriptors.*

| Criterion | Score | Weighted | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Content & structure | 4/5 | 24/30 | Three clear points in a good order, but 7:20 for a 10-minute slot means ~30% of content was cut live and the close was rushed |
| Visual design | 2/5 | 8/20 | Reading slides aloud is evidence they were text-heavy, not claim-based |
| Delivery | 2/5 | 10/25 | 14 "basically" in seven minutes ≈ two per minute; hands in pockets; pace ~175 wpm |
| Engagement & Q&A | 2/5 | 6/15 | Answered a different question than the one asked — the classic nervous-substitution error |

*Step 2 — Total:* 24 + 8 + 10 + 6 = 48 out of 75 on the four individual criteria; scaled to 100 → 48 / 75 × 100 = **64/100**.

*Step 3 — Three highest-leverage fixes, ranked by marks recovered.* (1) **Fix the clock** (~10 marks): rehearse twice with a visible timer and put the close on a card so it is never what gets sacrificed. (2) **Fix the Q&A substitution** (~6 marks): use
pause-repeat before answering, because repeating the question forces her to hear what was asked; practise five questions read out by a friend. (3) **Fix fillers and hands together** (~8 marks): replace "basically" with a half-second pause, and stand with hands
in the box for the whole of the next recorded run.

*Step 4 — What not to say:* not "your slides were bad" (personal, vague, no action), not "you were nervous" (a diagnosis she cannot act on), and not all eight problems at once — she can change one or two things per rehearsal.

**Why this works:** the feedback is **scored, ranked and quantified**, so it is arguable rather than a matter of taste; it targets the two or three changes with the biggest mark recovery instead of listing every flaw; and each item names a specific behaviour
with a specific next action, which is the only kind of feedback that changes anything.

⚠️ **Common trap:** rehearsing in your head, or silently. Both feel like practice and are not. Second trap: collecting feedback from ten people and changing everything at once — the talk gets worse and you learn nothing. Third: rehearsing with slides only and
never rehearsing the **Q&A**, which is often 15% of the marks for three minutes of work.

No formulas in this module — but here is the "which rule when?" guide you should recall in three seconds.

## Solved examples

### Example 1 (easy) — cut the deck to fit the clock

**Task:** You have 15 slides for a ten-minute slot: title, agenda, problem, three method slides, four result slides, two limitation slides, two future-work slides, comparison, conclusion, thank you. Reduce it to a correct deck.

**Solution:** Ten minutes → 7–10 content slides plus title, so target **nine including title**. Keep: title; **problem + roadmap** (the agenda slide is almost always wasted airtime — put the roadmap as three words on the problem slide); **one method diagram**
plus one text slide instead of three method slides; **two results** (accuracy and failure analysis), moving training curves to backup; **one limitations** slide with three bullets; **conclusion + future work merged** (they say the same thing); a minimal
thank-you slide. Result: title, problem+roadmap, method diagram, method detail, results A, results B, limitations, conclusion+future, thank you = **nine slides**. Nine slides removed, zero claims removed — the training curves, extra method detail and comparison
table become **backup slides** after the thank-you, used only if asked.

**Final answer:** nine slides for ten minutes, plus four to six backup slides in reserve. Deleting an agenda slide and a duplicated conclusion is the cheapest two minutes you will ever recover.

### Example 2 (medium) — from paragraph to slide plus speaker notes

**Task:** Convert this into a slide plus speaker notes: *"Our system also has a teacher dashboard where the teacher can see who was marked present, who was marked absent, who was unrecognised, and can correct any entry manually, and all of this is stored in a
database so it can be exported to Excel at the end of the month for the office."*

**Solution:** *Find the single claim* — the dashboard is not a feature list; the claim is "the teacher stays in control".

```text
Title:  The teacher can override anything
[ PRESENT ]        [ ABSENT ]         [ UNSURE ]
 auto-marked        auto-marked        flagged for review
footer: Every entry editable. One-click export to Excel at month end.
```

*Speaker notes (≈45 words, about 20 s):* "The important design choice: we never make the system the final authority. Anyone unrecognised goes into an 'unsure' bucket, not an 'absent' one — because marking a present student absent is a far worse error than asking
the teacher to look. The teacher can edit any row, and the month's sheet exports in one click."

**Final answer:** one slide carrying the claim (teacher in control), three labelled states instead of a bullet list, and the reasoning moved to speaker notes. The insight — *"unsure" is not "absent"* — becomes sayable because it is no longer buried inside a
sentence.

### Example 3 (exam level) — full marking-scheme self-evaluation

**Task:** Score this 90-second opening against the 5.7 rubric (content 30, design 20, delivery 25, Q&A 15), justify each score, and name the two changes that recover the most marks: *"Okay so, um, good morning everyone, I'm Priya and this is my presentation on
our project which is a chatbot for the college website. So basically the problem is that students ask a lot of questions to the office and the office is busy, so we made a chatbot. It uses NLP. Um, the chatbot answers questions about fees, dates, and hostel. We
used a transformer model, and, uh, we got good results. So, yeah, in this presentation I will talk about the problem, the solution, the results, and the conclusion. Thank you."*

**Solution:** *Content:* a problem-solution-results-conclusion roadmap genuinely exists, which earns credit, but there is no number anywhere ("a lot of questions", "good results" are impressions), the roadmap arrives at second 90 instead of second 10, and "it
uses NLP" is a label, not a claim → **2/5 = 12/30**. *Design:* not judgeable from audio alone — state that, and score the observable proxy: a bare "NLP" label suggests the slide said only "NLP", and "so yeah" as a closer suggests no designed closing slide →
**2/5 = 8/20 (provisional)**. *Delivery:* fillers — *um* ×3, *uh* ×1, *so / so yeah* ×3, *basically* ×1 = eight in 90 s; 105 words in 90 s ≈ **70 wpm**, far below 130–150, slow *and* filler-heavy, which usually means reciting from memory and stalling; no pause
after any claim → **2/5 = 10/25**. *Q&A:* none in the segment; engagement proxy — no "you", no rhetorical question, no reference to the audience's world → **1/5 = 3/15**.

*Total:* 12 + 8 + 10 + 3 = **33/90 (≈37%)**, a clear fail band — worth knowing, because students routinely assume a confident-sounding opening is a good one.

*Two highest-value changes.* (1) **Put a number in the first 15 seconds and move the roadmap to second 10** (~+12 across content and engagement): replace "students ask a lot of questions" with the real count — "the office answers about 120 fee queries a month,
and 70 of them are the same five questions". (2) **Halve the word count to remove the filler space** (~+10 in delivery): cut "so basically", "so yeah", "which is", and the redundant "in this presentation I will talk about the problem, the solution, the results,
and the conclusion" → "three things: what we built, what it gets right, what it gets wrong."

**Final answer:** 33/90 (≈37%) on the observed criteria; the two changes worth most are *quantify the hook and move the roadmap forward* (+12) and *cut the words to remove the filler space* (+10); projected post-fix score ≈55/90.

## Common traps and mistakes

- **Content dump instead of argument.** Listing everything you did instead of arguing three points costs Content marks and causes overrun, which costs Delivery marks. Fix: one-line message first.
- **Improvising the opening and close.** These carry the decision and the marks, yet they are what students leave unscripted. Fix: script both, 60 seconds each.
- **No time budget.** You discover the overrun live, then sprint. Fix: word budget = minutes × 140, then cut content — never speak faster.
- **Text-wall slides read aloud.** The audience reads ahead, finishes and disengages, and reading flattens your voice. Fix: claim on the slide, reasoning in your mouth.
- **6x6 applied to everything.** A results table or pipeline diagram legitimately breaks it. Fix: treat 6x6 as a guardrail against paragraphs, not a law.
- **Wrong chart for the question.** A nine-slice pie or a 3-D bar chart is a visible design error. Fix: match chart to the audience's question.
- **Fillers treated as a personality problem.** They are a pause problem. Fix: replace with silence; record and listen at 1.25x.
- **Memorising the whole script.** One forgotten word takes the paragraph with it. Fix: memorise only the first and last minute.
- **Jargon with no definition.** You lose the non-specialist half of the room and look unable to explain your own work. Fix: analogy ladder.
- **Bluffing in Q&A.** The most damaging move available, because examiners ask follow-ups by design. Fix: four honest bridges.
- **Arguing with a hostile questioner.** Even when right, you lose composure marks. Fix: concede the true part, answer once, time-box.
- **"…and now my teammate will continue."** Empty handover; the seam becomes visible. Fix: handoff sandwich.
- **Unbalanced airtime.** Visible on the clock and marked under teamwork. Fix: equal minutes, shared bookends.
- **Rehearsing silently or in your head.** Feels like practice, trains nothing. Fix: out loud, standing, timed, once recorded.
- **Vague feedback loops.** "It was good" teaches nothing. Fix: ask "where did you lose me?", change one thing per run.

## Quick revision box

- ✅ **Structure:** one-line message → three body points → 4-beat opening (hook, connect, purpose, roadmap) → 3-beat close (summary, takeaway, ask). Say the message before, during and after.
- ✅ **Time:** 10 min = 1 open + 6.5 body + 1.5 close + 1 buffer; at 130–150 wpm that is 1,300–1,500 words; 7–10 content slides.
- ✅ **Slides:** one idea each, 6x6 as a guardrail, body ≥18 pt, high contrast, three colours, no 3-D, never read aloud.
- ✅ **Charts:** time → line; comparison → bar; share of whole → pie (≤5 slices); exact values → table. Label axes and units.
- ✅ **Delivery:** 130–150 wpm; power pause 2–3 s after each claim; three eye anchors held 3–5 s; hands in the waist-to-shoulder box; feet planted.
- ✅ **Audiences:** analogy ladder — general (no jargon), peer (defined jargon), expert (baselines + limitations). Say where an analogy breaks.
- ✅ **Q&A:** pause → repeat → answer claim-first → check. Don't know? "I don't have that number; I'll send it tomorrow." Never invent a figure.
- ✅ **Teams & rehearsal:** split by complete ideas, equal minutes, handoff sandwich, written Q&A map; three timed runs minimum, one recorded, one change per run.

## Practice questions

**Q1 (easy).** Write the four beats of an opening for a five-minute talk on "Why should first-years learn Git?", with one line of actual script per beat.

**Q2 (easy).** Rewrite this bullet so the slide obeys one-idea-per-slide and 6x6: "Our app has three modules, namely the login module which uses OTP authentication for security, the dashboard module which shows attendance statistics in graphical form, and the
admin module which manages student records and can add or delete students and also export data to Excel."

**Q3 (medium).** Your ten-minute talk ran to 12:30 in rehearsal and you cannot cut content. Give three legitimate options and the one option you must not take, with reasons.

**Q4 (medium).** A questioner says sharply: "This is just a college project. Why should anyone care?" Answer using the pause-repeat-answer-check loop, and name the one move that keeps your tone steady.

**Q5 (exam level).** You are one of four speakers with 12 minutes total, before two faculty, one industry guest and one non-technical coordinator. Write the time split, the exact handover line from speaker 2 to speaker 3, and what each speaker must *not* do.

**Q6 (exam level).** Explain your own semester project at three levels (about 50 / 110 / 160 words), then add the single sentence you would use if an examiner asked "what would break this?"

## Answers

**A1.** *Hook:* "Right now four of you in this room have a folder called `final_final_v2`. That folder is why you need Git." *Connect:* "Every group project this semester will lose work at least once, and it is entirely preventable." *Purpose:* "In five minutes
I'll show you the three commands that cover ninety per cent of what you'll ever need." *Roadmap:* "First what version control actually does; then the three commands; then how not to break your teammate's work." **Key step:** the roadmap names the three points in
order, in one breath, before you start point one.

**A2.** Three slides, one idea each. (1) *Title:* "Logging in takes one OTP, not a password" — body: "OTP to registered number / no password to forget". (2) *Title:* "The dashboard answers one question: who is attending?" — body: "per-class bars / monthly
trend". (3) *Title:* "Admins can fix any record" — body: "add, delete, export to Excel". **Key step:** three modules are three ideas, so they are three slides; "namely" and "and also" are the tell-tale words that a slide is carrying more than one idea.

**A3.** Options: (1) **cut content** — drop the weakest of the three body points and re-budget the clock; (2) **compress within a point** — move supporting detail to backup slides and answer it in Q&A; (3) **renegotiate scope out loud** — if the slot is
genuinely fixed and the content genuinely required, tell the organiser in advance, not the audience mid-talk. The thing you must not do is **speak faster** to fit: past about 175 wpm comprehension drops sharply and fillers increase, so you lose delivery marks
*and* the audience. **Key step:** 12:30 against a ten-minute slot means roughly 350 words too many — about one body point's worth.

**A4.** "You're asking whether this matters outside the college. [*pause*] Fair question. Three things: the method is the same one used in production attendance systems, the failure analysis is ours, and the cost number — zero rupees per classroom, because it
runs on a phone — is the part a school would actually care about. If you meant 'is this novel', then no, and I've said so on slide 4." The move that keeps your tone steady: **concede one true thing in the first two sentences**, because concession costs nothing
and removes the pressure to defend everything. **Key step:** answer once, then stop; do not open a second round.

**A5.** *Split:* three minutes each — Speaker 1 hook + problem + roadmap, Speaker 2 method, Speaker 3 results, Speaker 4 limitations + close + ask — with Speaker 1 returning for thirty seconds at the end to deliver the ask; state that explicitly. *Handover 2→3:*
"So the method is settled — two-stage, tuned per class. What that buys us is a number, and a number is only useful with its failures next to it. Kabir has both." *What each must not do:* Speaker 1 must not re-explain the problem in the close; Speaker 2 must not
read the pipeline diagram aloud; Speaker 3 must not quote accuracy without the test-set size; Speaker 4 must not introduce new content on the limitations slide. **Key step:** the handover is a *content bridge* (method → the number it produces), not a stage
direction.

**A6.** Model shape, substituting your own project. *General (~50 w):* "It's a camera at the door that marks attendance by recognising faces, like your phone unlocking — but for a whole class in forty seconds." *Peer (~110 w):* "Two stages: detect the face, then
convert it into a 128-number signature and match it against the class list by distance, with a per-class threshold. The threshold is the whole game — loose and proxies get in, tight and real students get rejected." *Expert (~160 w):* "RetinaFace-style detection
plus a FaceNet-style embedding, triplet loss, fine-tuned on 210 identities; cosine distance, threshold 0.42 chosen on validation by F1. Baselines: pixel k-NN 71%, pre-trained 86%, ours 94%. 62% of residual errors are low light, 21% occlusion. No liveness
detection, so a printed photo passes. Split by identity, to avoid leakage." *"What would break this?"* "Any change in deployment conditions — a new camera angle, different lighting, or masked faces — because the threshold was tuned on one condition, and it would
fail quietly rather than loudly, which is why low-confidence matches go to a review bucket instead of being marked absent." **Key step:** identical claims at all three rungs; only vocabulary, evidence density and the explicitness of limitations change.
