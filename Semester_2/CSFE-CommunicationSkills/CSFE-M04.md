## The big picture

Writing is the skill that gets *judged* the most and taught the least. In college you are marked on lab reports, assignments, seminar abstracts and answer scripts; in a job you will be judged on emails, design documents, incident reports and proposals. Nobody will grade your accent, but everybody will read your writing — and unlike speech, writing does not let you correct yourself with a smile.

Here is the mental model that fixes 80% of weak writing: **writing is not talking, typed out.** Speech is repaired on the fly by tone, gesture and "sorry, I meant…". Writing gets exactly one shot, read by someone tired, in a hurry, possibly not a native speaker of your context. So writing has to do the repair work in advance: a clear structure the reader can skim, sentences whose meaning cannot be misread, and a tone that does not accidentally sound rude or childish.

Think of a document as a building, not a sentence-factory:

- **Structure** (walls and floors) — sections, paragraphs, topic sentences. This is *coherence*: the reader always knows where they are.
- **Connections** (doors and staircases) — linking words, pronouns, repeated key terms. This is *cohesion*: the reader can move from one idea to the next without falling.
- **Finishing** (paint and plaster) — grammar, spelling, punctuation, formatting. This is *correctness*, and it is what a reader notices first even though it is the least important.

Most students polish the paint and forget the walls. That is why a grammatically perfect paragraph can still score badly: it never told the reader what it was about.

## Basics you must know first

- **Sentence** — one complete thought with a subject and a finite verb. A *fragment* ("Because the server crashed.") is not a sentence; a *run-on* ("The server crashed we lost data.") is two sentences fused without punctuation or a conjunction.
- **Paragraph** — one idea, developed. If a paragraph has two ideas, it is two paragraphs.
- **Register** — the formality level: chat message → email → report → research paper. Wrong register is the commonest "it feels off" problem.
- **Claim** — a statement you assert to be true. **Evidence** — what backs it. **Warrant** — the reasoning that connects the two.
- **Citation** — telling the reader where a fact or idea came from.
- **Draft** — a version written to be improved, not defended. Every good document is a second draft.

## 4.1 Extended writing

### Intuition

A paragraph is a thought; an essay is an argument made of thoughts. The difference is not length — it is that an essay must *go somewhere*. A student who writes six paragraphs, each a separate observation about social media, has written six paragraphs, not an essay.

The one-sentence test: **can you state what your essay argues, in one sentence, before you write it?** If yes, every paragraph has a job (support, qualify, illustrate that sentence). If no, you are hoping the argument appears while typing, and it usually does not.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Write the thesis statement first, and make it arguable.** A thesis is the claim the whole text defends. It must be specific enough that someone could disagree with it.

```text
Weak (a topic, not a thesis): "This essay is about online examinations."
Weak (unarguable):            "Online examinations have advantages and disadvantages."
Strong (arguable, specific):  "Online examinations are fairer than written ones for large
                               first-year courses, provided the question paper is redesigned
                               for open-book conditions."
```

The strong version tells you what to prove and hints at the counter-argument (invigilation, cheating) that you must answer.

**Step 2 — Plan in a skeleton, not in prose.** Five minutes of planning saves twenty minutes of rewriting. A workable skeleton for a 700-word essay:

```text
Thesis: <one sentence>
P1  Background + why the question matters (2-3 sentences, no argument yet)
P2  Claim 1 + evidence + explanation + link to thesis
P3  Claim 2 + evidence + explanation + link to thesis
P4  Counter-claim + rebuttal (this is what separates a 6/10 from an 8/10)
P5  Conclusion: restate thesis in new words + what follows from it (no new claims)
```

**Step 3 — Build every body paragraph with PEEL.** **P**oint (topic sentence stating the claim of *this* paragraph), **E**vidence (data, example, quotation, your own observation), **E**xplanation (why the evidence supports the point — this is the part students skip and it is where marks live), **L**ink (back to the thesis, or forward to the next paragraph).

Model PEEL paragraph:

> **Point:** Redesigned open-book papers test understanding rather than recall. **Evidence:** In a
> 2022 trial across three engineering colleges, students given open-book papers scored 14% lower on
> recall questions but 22% higher on questions requiring the application of a formula to an unfamiliar
> case. **Explanation:** This shift matters because recall is the skill employers automate and
> application is the skill they hire for; a closed-book paper rewards memorising a formula's
> statement, while an open-book paper rewards knowing when to use it. **Link:** If the assessment is
> changed to match the skill we claim to teach, the fairness objection to online exams loses most of
> its force.

**Step 4 — Control paragraph length and shape.** 100–180 words per paragraph in academic writing; one idea per paragraph; the first sentence carries the claim, the last sentence either closes it or hands over to the next.

**Step 5 — Write the introduction and conclusion last.** They are summaries of an argument you have not built yet. Write them after the body exists, and the introduction will actually match the essay.

**Step 6 — Revise at three levels, in this order.** (1) *Argument*: is the thesis answered, is any paragraph doing no work? (2) *Structure*: does each paragraph open with a claim, do the links exist? (3) *Language*: grammar, word choice, spelling. Fixing language first is the most common waste of time — you may delete the whole paragraph anyway.

**Example question:** Write one PEEL paragraph (about 105 words) answering: "Should first-year students be allowed to use AI assistants for programming assignments?"

**Solution:**

1. **Choose a position you can defend in one paragraph** — not the whole debate. *Reason: one paragraph carries one claim; a paragraph that argues both sides argues neither.*
2. **P (Point):** "Allowing AI assistants in first-year programming assignments is defensible only if the assessment is changed to test debugging and verification rather than code production."
3. **E (Evidence):** "Students who copy a working solution from an assistant typically cannot explain why it works: in a small classroom study, 8 of 12 students who submitted AI-generated code could not locate the bug when the same code was deliberately broken." *Reason: a concrete observation beats a general claim.*
4. **E (Explanation):** "The value of the assignment was never the typing — it was the mental model built while debugging. If the deliverable is code, the assistant removes the learning; if the deliverable is a verified, tested, explained solution, the assistant becomes a fast compiler that still requires a programmer." *Reason: this is the step students skip, and it is where the marks sit.*
5. **L (Link):** "The question is therefore not whether to ban the tool, but what the assignment is actually measuring."
6. **Check length and shape.** The paragraph is about 105 words, opens with a claim, contains one piece of evidence, explains it, and ends by re-framing the question. *Reason: PEEL is a shape, and shape is checkable.*

**Final answer:** the paragraph above — Point (assessment must change), Evidence (8 of 12 could not debug), Explanation (the learning was in the debugging), Link (re-frame the question).

**Why this works:** it makes one claim instead of surveying a debate, it separates evidence from interpretation, and the link sentence makes the paragraph do work for a larger argument rather than standing alone.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Writing an "essay" that is really a list — one paragraph per example, no thesis, no links. It feels organised because the paragraphs are tidy, but there is no argument, so it scores in the middle no matter how good the grammar is. Second trap: a thesis so vague ("technology has changed education") that no evidence could ever contradict it.

## 4.2 Technical reports, proposals and professional documents

### Intuition

A **report** looks backwards: here is what we did, what we found, and what it means. A **proposal** looks forwards: here is what we propose to do, why it is worth funding, and what it will cost. Same skeleton, opposite tense and opposite purpose — and mixing them is one of the most common reasons a project proposal gets rejected ("you have written a report on work you have not done yet").

Professional documents are the same skill at smaller scale: an email is a one-page document with a subject line doing the work of a title. The reader's question is always "what do you want from me, and by when?" — answer it in the first two lines.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Use the standard report structure.** Markers look for these sections, and so do managers:

```text
Title page        : title, author, roll number, date, course
Abstract / Exec summary : 100-150 words, the whole report in miniature (problem, method, result)
1. Introduction   : background, problem, aim, scope (what is NOT covered)
2. Literature / Related work : what others found (short in a course report)
3. Methodology    : how you did it, enough for someone to repeat it
4. Results        : what you measured, in tables and figures, no interpretation yet
5. Discussion     : what the results mean, limitations, sources of error
6. Conclusion     : answer the aim in 3-5 sentences
7. Recommendations: what should be done next (optional in a lab report, expected in a survey)
References        : every source you actually used
Appendices        : raw data, code listings, questionnaires
```

**Step 2 — Separate results from discussion.** Results report; discussion interprets. "The average latency was 42 ms" belongs in Results; "this is acceptable for interactive use but not for real-time control" belongs in Discussion. Mixing them makes the report look like an opinion piece.

**Step 3 — Know the report-vs-proposal differences.**

| Feature | Report | Proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Time direction | past (what was done) | future (what will be done) |
| Main verb tense | past ("we measured") | future/present ("we will measure") |
| Core question | what happened, and what does it mean? | why fund this, and can you deliver it? |
| Must contain | results, discussion, limitations | objectives, methodology, timeline, budget, outcomes |
| Reader's decision | accept the findings | release money, time or approval |
| Failure mode | results without interpretation | promises without a plan or a cost |

**Step 4 — Write emails with the ask in the first two lines.** Structure: subject line that names the topic and the action → one-line context → the specific ask → the deadline → what you have attached. Three samples:

```text
REQUEST
Subject: Request for lab slot change - CS lab, Thursday 2 pm

Respected Sir/Madam,
I am Ravi Kumar (22CS1043), Section B. I have a mid-semester exam on Thursday at 2 pm,
which clashes with our DBMS lab slot.
Could our batch's lab be moved to Friday 10 am, or may I attend the Friday batch instead?
I have attached the exam timetable for reference.
Thank you for considering this.
Ravi Kumar, 22CS1043

ESCALATION (after a reasonable wait, with facts and no blame)
Subject: Escalation: lab network down for 3 days - impact on Batch B submissions

Respected Sir,
The CS lab network has been unavailable since Monday 9 am, so Batch B (32 students) cannot
access the DBMS server for the assignment due on Friday.
I reported this to the lab assistant on Monday (ticket 1187) and again on Tuesday; the
issue is still open.
Could the deadline be extended to Monday, or could we be given access to the backup server?
I can be reached on 98xxxxxx21 at any time today.
Ravi Kumar, 22CS1043, Batch B

APOLOGY (own it, say what you will do, no excuses first)
Subject: Apology and correction: wrong file attached to yesterday's submission

Respected Sir,
I attached the wrong PDF to yesterday's assignment submission; the correct file is attached
here and is also uploaded to the portal as "assignment2_final.pdf".
I have checked the remaining pages and the rest of the submission is unaffected.
I am sorry for the extra work this causes you, and I will verify attachments before
submitting from now on.
Ravi Kumar, 22CS1043
```

**Step 5 — Format a formal letter correctly.** Sender's address, date, receiver's address, subject, salutation, body (3 paragraphs: purpose, detail, action), complimentary close, signature block. Two conventions worth knowing (British style): `Dear Sir/Madam` closes with `Yours faithfully`; `Dear Mr Sharma` closes with `Yours sincerely`. Do not mix them.

**Step 6 — Use a memo for internal, no-salutation communication.** Format: `TO / FROM / DATE / SUBJECT`, then a one-paragraph purpose statement, then numbered points, then the action required. No "Dear", no signature.

**Example question:** You are the team lead of a four-person mini-project group. A teammate has missed two review meetings and his module is three days late, which now threatens the submission. Write an escalation email to the course instructor (about 105 words) and state what you deliberately did *not* write.

**Solution:**

1. **Subject line names topic + impact + action.** "Escalation: Module 2 delayed by 3 days - risk to Friday submission (Team 7)". *Reason: the instructor must be able to prioritise from the inbox list alone.*
2. **Line 1–2: who, what, which team.** "I am Ananya Rao (22CS1071), lead of Team 7 in the Software Engineering mini-project." *Reason: context before complaint; the reader should not have to search.*
3. **Facts and dates, no adjectives.** "Module 2 (database layer) was due on Tuesday. It is now three days late, and the teammate responsible has not attended the last two review meetings (5 and 12 November)." *Reason: dates are checkable; "he is careless" is not, and it makes you look unprofessional.*
4. **Impact, quantified.** "Because Module 3 depends on it, our integration and testing window has shrunk from five days to two, and the Friday submission is at risk." *Reason: escalation is justified by impact, not by frustration.*
5. **Two concrete options, not an open complaint.** "Could you advise on (a) a short extension for the team, or (b) reassigning Module 2 to another member this week? I have kept a record of the review minutes in case they are useful." *Reason: giving options makes it easy to act, which is the only thing escalation is for.*
6. **What I deliberately did not write.** No blame adjectives ("irresponsible"), no group chat screenshots, no threat, no CC to five people, no "as I already told you". *Reason: escalation is a request for a decision; anything else invites a reply about your tone instead of the problem.*

**Final answer:** the email above — subject with impact, identity and context, dated facts, quantified risk, two actionable options, professional close — and deliberately no blame, no sarcasm, no mass CC.

**Why this works:** it converts a personal frustration into a decision someone can make in thirty seconds, and it keeps a record that protects the team rather than attacking a person.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Writing an escalation in the heat of the moment, with "kindly look into the matter at the earliest" and no dates or impact — it reads as a complaint and gets ignored. Another trap: the report that has Results and Conclusion but no Discussion, so the reader cannot tell what the numbers mean; and the proposal written in the past tense about work not yet done.

## 4.3 Describing data and visuals

### Intuition

A chart is an argument in pictures, and your paragraph is the caption that tells the reader what to notice. Two rules follow. First, **never describe every number** — a reader can see the bars; what they cannot see is which difference matters. Second, **give the overview before the details**: "all four states improved, and the gap between them narrowed" is more useful than any single percentage.

Think of it as telling someone about a cricket match: you say who won and by how much first, then the innings that decided it — not ball-by-ball from over one.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Use precise trend verbs and adverbs.** Build a small vocabulary and vary it; repeating "increased" six times makes a description sound mechanical.

| Direction | Verbs | Adverbs / degree | Nouns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up | rose, climbed, increased, grew, surged, soared | slightly, steadily, sharply, dramatically, twofold | an increase, a rise, growth, an upturn |
| Down | fell, dropped, declined, decreased, dipped, plummeted | marginally, gradually, steeply, sharply | a fall, a decline, a drop, a downturn |
| Flat | plateaued, levelled off, remained stable, held steady | at around, roughly, approximately | a plateau, stability |
| Changeable | fluctuated, oscillated, varied | erratically, wildly | fluctuation |
| Multiples | doubled, tripled, quadrupled, halved | nearly, almost, exactly | a doubling, a halving |
| Comparison | exceeded, overtook, lagged behind, matched, was twice that of | considerably, marginally | the highest, the lowest, the gap |

**Step 2 — Follow the four-part structure: introduce → overview → details → (optional) close.**
Introduction: what the chart shows (one sentence, no numbers). Overview: the two or three biggest patterns. Details: the numbers that support those patterns, grouped logically (by item, or by time). Close (only in longer texts): the implication.

**Step 3 — Group, do not list.** "State A rose fastest, from 12% to 71%, while D rose slowest, from 25% to 52%" is a comparison. "A was 12, then 38, then 71; B was 20, then 45, then 66…" is a list, and it wastes your word limit.

**Step 4 — Handle time and units precisely.** Use past tense for finished periods ("fell in 2020"), present perfect for periods continuing to now ("has risen since 2019"), and always attach the unit (% of households, ₹ crore, ms). Never write "increased by 2 times"; write "doubled", or "increased twofold".

**Step 5 — Name what is *not* shown.** A strong description adds one honest caveat ("the chart covers households, not individual users"), because it shows you understood the data rather than just reading labels.

**Example question:** Describe the chart below in about 150 words, using the overview-first structure. Data: percentage of households with internet access.

| State | 2015 | 2019 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 12 | 38 | 71 |
| B | 20 | 45 | 66 |
| C | 8 | 22 | 58 |
| D | 25 | 40 | 52 |

**Solution (model description, 150 words):**

> The table shows the percentage of households with internet access in four states (A–D) at three
> points between 2015 and 2023.
>
> Overall, access expanded in every state, but the gap between the best- and worst-covered states
> widened slightly, from 17 to 19 percentage points, because the leading state also grew fastest.
>
> State A recorded the fastest growth, rising almost sixfold from 12% in 2015 to 71% in 2023, which
> lifted it from second-lowest to the highest position. State C followed a similar path, climbing
> from 8% to 58%, a more than sevenfold increase from the smallest base. By contrast, State D had the
> widest coverage in 2015 at 25% but grew most slowly, finishing last at 52%. State B stayed in the
> middle throughout, roughly tripling from 20% to 66%. The final period was the stronger one for A
> and C, which added 33 and 36 percentage points respectively after 2019.

Step-by-step reasons (each one is checkable against the table):

1. **Sentence 1 fixes what is measured and when** — no numbers yet. *Reason: the reader must know what
   the unit is before any figure appears.*
2. **Sentence 2 is the overview**, and it is arithmetic-checked: 2015 spread = 25 − 8 = 17 points;
   2023 spread = 71 − 52 = 19 points. *Reason: an overview claim that the numbers do not support is
   the costliest error in this task, so compute the gap before writing about it.*
3. **Details are grouped, not listed**: fastest (A), similar (C), slowest (D), middle (B). *Reason: a
   grouped description answers "what should I notice?"; a row-by-row list only repeats the table.*
4. **Multiples are computed, not guessed**: 71 / 12 ≈ 5.9 (almost sixfold), 58 / 8 ≈ 7.25 (more than
   sevenfold), 66 / 20 = 3.3 (roughly tripled). *Reason: markers check these.*
5. **One closing observation** ("the final period was stronger for A and C": 71 − 38 = 33 and
   58 − 22 = 36) *Reason: it adds something the reader cannot see at a glance, which is the only
   justification for a fifth sentence.*

**Final answer:** the 150-word description above. Its spine is: what the chart shows → overview
(everything rose, gap widened from 17 to 19 points) → grouped details with computed multiples → one
closing observation.

**Why this works:** the overview carries the mark, the details are grouped so each sentence compares
rather than lists, and every figure in it can be verified against the table in under a minute.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Describing data you did not check. Percentage *points* are not per cent: a rise
from 12% to 71% is 59 percentage points, and it is also roughly a sixfold increase — mixing the two
produces sentences that are confidently wrong. Second trap: starting with "The chart given below
shows…" and spending 60 of your 150 words on the introduction.

## 4.4 Coherence and cohesion

### Intuition

Two words that sound alike and are graded separately. **Coherence** is whether the ideas hold together — the reader can follow the argument from paragraph to paragraph. **Cohesion** is whether the sentences are stitched together — the reader can follow from sentence to sentence without stumbling.

Think of a train: coherence is the route (does this train go somewhere I want to go?), cohesion is the couplings between coaches (will the coaches stay attached?). A document with perfect couplings but no route is a beautifully linked list of unrelated points; a document with a great route but no couplings feels like jumping between coaches.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Make every paragraph start with its claim.** A topic sentence is not decoration; it is the reader's map. If you delete the first sentence of each paragraph and the argument still works, your topic sentences were decorative.

**Step 2 — Use linking words by *function*, not by habit.**

| Function | Linking words | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | and, moreover, furthermore, in addition, also | "Moreover, the sample was small." |
| Contrast | but, however, yet, although, whereas, in contrast, nevertheless | "The mean rose; however, the median fell." |
| Cause | because, since, as, due to, owing to | "Because the buffer overflowed, frames were dropped." |
| Effect | therefore, thus, hence, consequently, as a result, so | "The cache was cold; consequently, latency doubled." |
| Sequence | first, then, next, subsequently, finally, meanwhile | "First the model is trained; then it is quantised." |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, namely | "Two metrics, namely precision and recall, were reported." |
| Concession | although, even though, while it is true that | "Although costs fell, adoption stayed flat." |
| Conclusion | in conclusion, overall, to sum up, in short | "Overall, the intervention helped only in large classes." |

**Step 3 — Reference backwards instead of repeating.** Use pronouns and summary nouns: `this`, `these`, `such`, `the former / the latter`, `this finding`, `that assumption`. The rule: a reference word must point to something unambiguous and recent.

```text
Repeating : The compiler rejected the file. The compiler also logged a warning about the file.
Cohesive  : The compiler rejected the file and logged a warning about it.
Ambiguous : The compiler rejected the parser's output because it was outdated.   <- "it" = compiler or output?
```

**Step 4 — Repeat key terms on purpose.** Exact repetition of a technical term is *good* cohesion; using three synonyms for the same thing ("the model / the system / the framework") makes the reader wonder whether you mean three things. This is the opposite of school advice about "avoid repetition" — that advice is for essays about holidays, not for technical writing.

**Step 5 — Do not over-link.** Every "moreover" and "furthermore" costs the reader attention. Two rules: do not use a linking word when the logical relation is already obvious from the sentence order, and never start more than two consecutive sentences with one.

**Example question:** Rewrite this paragraph so it is coherent and cohesive, then list what you changed.

> "Solar panels are cheap now. Many houses have them. The government gives a subsidy. The subsidy is
> only for residential use. Commercial buildings do not get it. Commercial buildings use more
> electricity. The policy may be redesigned."

**Solution:**

1. **Find the through-line.** The paragraph's real point is a mismatch: the subsidy targets the smaller consumer while the bigger consumer is excluded. *Reason: coherence starts by naming the claim, then ordering sentences to serve it.*
2. **Write the topic sentence:** "India's rooftop solar subsidy is aimed at the wrong consumer." *Reason: the reader now knows what every following sentence is doing.*
3. **Group the sentences into two moves: what the policy does, then why that is a problem.** "The subsidy covers residential rooftops only. Yet commercial buildings, which consume far more electricity per square metre, are excluded."
4. **Add only the links that carry logic** — one contrast marker (`yet`), one consequence (`as a result`). *Reason: over-linking is as bad as no linking.*
5. **Use reference words instead of repetition:** "the subsidy" → "it"; "commercial buildings" repeated once is fine because it is the key term.

Model rewrite: "India's rooftop solar subsidy is aimed at the wrong consumer. Panels are now cheap enough for most middle-class households, and the subsidy covers residential rooftops only. Yet commercial buildings, which consume far more electricity per square metre, are excluded. As a result, the policy subsidises the smaller load and leaves the larger one on the grid."

**Final answer:** the rewrite above. Changes made: (1) added a topic sentence stating the claim, (2) grouped six short sentences into two logical moves, (3) replaced repetition with `it` and `the policy`, (4) added exactly two connectives (`Yet`, `As a result`), (5) kept the technical key terms unreplaced.

**Why this works:** the paragraph now has a route (claim → what the policy does → why that fails) and couplings that only appear where the logic turns, so the reader spends attention on the argument instead of on decoding.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Sprinkling connectives as decoration — "Moreover, furthermore, besides this, in addition to this" in four consecutive sentences. It signals "I know linking words exist", not "I have a logical relation here". If you can delete the word and the logic survives, delete it.

## 4.5 Formal and objective writing

### Intuition

Formal writing is not "fancy writing". It is writing that removes the writer's personality from the foreground so the content can occupy it. Compare a lab notebook entry and a WhatsApp message about the same event: the facts are identical, but one is about the experiment and the other is about you.

Objectivity has a practical purpose in engineering: a report must be usable by someone who does not trust you, does not know you, and will check your numbers. "I think the motor overheated" is a claim about your mental state; "the motor winding reached 118 °C, above the 105 °C insulation rating" is a claim about the world.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Use the passive when the *action* matters more than the actor.** Passive = `be` + past participle. It is right when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, obvious, or when you deliberately want to foreground the object.

```text
Active  : We heated the samples to 60 °C for 20 minutes.
Passive : The samples were heated to 60 °C for 20 minutes.        <- better in a method section
Active  : The intern deleted the database.
Passive : The database was deleted.                               <- hides who did it: use only if that is honest
```

Style note, not a rule: many journals and most Indian engineering reports prefer the passive in Methods. Some modern journals and most software documentation prefer active ("we trained the model"). Ask your department which it wants; both are defensible. What is never acceptable is using the passive to avoid responsibility.

**Step 2 — Hedge claims to match your evidence.** Hedging is not weakness; it is accuracy about how much you actually know.

| Overclaim | Hedged | Why the hedge is more accurate |
|---|---|---|
| "This proves that X causes Y." | "This suggests that X may contribute to Y." | one study cannot prove causation |
| "The system is completely secure." | "The system resists the attacks tested in this study." | absolute claims are untestable |
| "All students struggle with grammar." | "Most students in the sample struggled with articles." | scope and quantity are stated |
| "Results will improve." | "Results are likely to improve." | prediction, not fact |

Hedges to keep handy: `may`, `appears to`, `suggests`, `indicates`, `tends to`, `is likely to`, `in this sample`, `under these conditions`.

**Step 3 — Remove first person and contractions from reports (a style convention, not grammar).** `I think`, `we feel`, `I guess` → delete or convert. `don't`, `can't`, `it's` → `do not`, `cannot`, `it is`. Note: "we" is normal in engineering papers when it means "the authors"; "I think" is not, because a report is not about your thinking.

**Step 4 — Nominalise to make the text dense (and stop when it becomes unreadable).** Turning verbs into nouns raises formality: "We analysed the data and found that…" → "Analysis of the data showed that…". Overdone, it produces "the implementation of the optimisation of the allocation", which is worse than the plain version. Density is a tool, not a target.

**Step 5 — Cut conversational intensifiers and vague quantifiers.** `very`, `really`, `a lot of`, `some`, `things`, `etc.` — replace with numbers or delete. "There were a lot of errors in many files" → "Seventeen files contained 43 errors."

**Before/after rewrites (the whole sub-topic in one table):**

| Weak (informal / subjective) | Strong (formal / objective) | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| "I think our project is pretty good and it works most of the time." | "The prototype met all five functional requirements; one non-functional requirement, cold-start latency, remains unmet." | opinion → measured claim; hedge removed by evidence |
| "We didn't get good results because the lab was closed a lot." | "Testing was limited by lab availability; only 6 of 12 planned trials were completed." | blame → factual constraint |
| "The results are very bad for large inputs." | "Accuracy fell from 94% to 71% for inputs above 10,000 records." | vague → quantified |
| "You can see from the graph that sales went up a lot." | "Sales rose 38% between Q2 and Q4 (Figure 3)." | reader-directed → data-directed; figure cited |
| "It is obvious that the algorithm is better." | "The algorithm reduced runtime by 31% on the same hardware." | "obvious" → evidence |
| "Mistakes were made during testing." | "Two test cases were skipped when the test rig failed." | responsibility dodged → named honestly |

**Example question:** Rewrite this paragraph as the Method section of a lab report (about 70 words): "So we took 5 resistors and I measured them with the multimeter, and honestly the readings were all over the place, maybe because the multimeter is old. We didn't really do the temperature part because there was no time."

**Solution:**

1. **Delete the narrator.** "So we", "I measured", "honestly" → remove. *Reason: a method section describes the procedure, not the person.*
2. **Convert to passive where the actor is irrelevant:** "Five resistors were measured using a digital multimeter." *Reason: the reader needs to repeat the procedure, not meet you.*
3. **Quantify the vague:** "all over the place" → give the spread; "maybe because the multimeter is old" → state it as a possible source of error with a fact attached. "The readings varied by up to 4%, which may reflect the multimeter's calibration status (last calibrated 2019)." *Reason: "maybe" is a feeling; a calibration date is evidence.*
4. **State the omission factually, in the limitations:** "The temperature-dependence test was not performed because the apparatus was unavailable within the lab session." *Reason: an honest limitation costs nothing; a hidden one destroys the report.*
5. **Check tense and voice consistency.** All past tense, all passive or impersonal. *Reason: mixing tenses inside a procedure reads as carelessness.*

Model rewrite: "Five carbon-film resistors (1 kΩ, 5%) were measured with a digital multimeter (model DT-830D, last calibrated 2019). Each resistor was measured three times and the mean recorded. Readings varied by up to 4% across repetitions, which may reflect the meter's calibration status rather than the components. The temperature-dependence test was not performed, as the heating apparatus was unavailable during the session; this limits the results to room-temperature behaviour."

**Final answer:** the rewrite above — impersonal, past tense, quantified, with the omission declared as a limitation instead of an excuse.

**Why this works:** every sentence is checkable by a stranger, and nothing in it depends on trusting the writer's mood or memory.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Thinking "formal" means long words. "Utilise" for "use", "prior to" for "before", "in order to" for "to" make writing longer without making it more formal — markers call this *pseudo-formal* writing. The other trap is the passive used to dodge responsibility ("mistakes were made"), which readers notice immediately.

## 4.6 Argumentative and certification-style writing

### Intuition

An argument is not a fight; it is a structure with three moving parts. You **claim** something, you acknowledge the strongest **counter-claim**, and you **rebut** it. Skip the counter-claim and your writing sounds naive; skip the rebuttal and you have conceded. The rebuttal is the part that earns the top band in IELTS/TOEFL essays, because it shows you can hold two ideas at once.

Certification writing is the same skill under a clock: IELTS Writing Task 2 is 250 words in about 40 minutes, Task 1 (a chart description) 150 words in about 20 minutes; TOEFL's independent essay is about 300 words in 30 minutes. The examiner is not looking for brilliance; they are looking for a clear position, developed paragraphs, and range with accuracy.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Build the claim–counter-claim–rebuttal unit.**

```text
Claim        : Remote work raises productivity for focused engineering tasks.
Counter-claim: But it weakens the informal mentoring that junior engineers need.
Rebuttal     : That loss is real but fixable by design - scheduled pairing sessions and code
               reviews replace the corridor conversation; what cannot be replaced is the
               accidental learning, which argues for hybrid rather than fully remote work.
```

Note the shape of a good rebuttal: it *concedes* what is true, *limits* the damage with a mechanism, and *narrows* the claim rather than denying the objection.

**Step 2 — Use the certification essay structure.**

```text
Introduction (2-3 sentences) : paraphrase the question, state your position clearly
Body 1                       : strongest reason + example + explanation
Body 2                       : second reason, OR the counter-claim + rebuttal
Conclusion (2 sentences)     : restate position, no new arguments, no new examples
```

**Step 3 — Take a position and keep it.** "While there are advantages and disadvantages, it depends on the situation" is the classic band-6 answer: it is a refusal to answer. You may take a *qualified* position ("beneficial, provided that…") — that is different from refusing.

**Step 4 — Write to the clock in fixed blocks.** For a 40-minute, 250-word essay: 5 minutes planning (position + two reasons + one example each), 30 minutes writing, 5 minutes checking articles, plurals and verb endings. Never leave checking to chance — it is worth more marks per minute than writing.

**Step 5 — Add range deliberately.** One complex sentence, one conditional, one concessive (`although`), one impersonal construction. Do not attempt range you cannot control: an accurate simple sentence scores higher than a broken complex one.

**Example question:** Write a body paragraph (about 130 words) for: "Universities should stop ranking students by marks and use continuous assessment instead." Include a counter-claim and a rebuttal.

**Solution:**

1. **Claim first, in the topic sentence:** "Continuous assessment is fairer than a single final examination because it measures learning rather than performance on one day." *Reason: the paragraph's job must be visible in sentence one.*
2. **Evidence with a mechanism:** "A student who falls ill during exam week loses a whole semester's grade, whereas coursework spread across twelve weeks makes any single bad week recoverable." *Reason: mechanisms persuade; adjectives do not.*
3. **Counter-claim, stated fairly:** "The obvious objection is that continuous assessment rewards diligence over understanding, and that marks awarded for weekly submissions can be inflated by effort rather than accuracy." *Reason: state the objection in its strongest form, or the rebuttal is worthless.*
4. **Rebuttal that concedes and narrows:** "This is partly true, but it describes a design flaw rather than an inherent limit: assessment that uses unseen in-class tasks and clear rubrics measures understanding while keeping the spread of effort. What continuous assessment genuinely loses is the single high-pressure performance, which is precisely the skill universities claim not to be teaching." *Reason: concede, then relocate the problem.*
5. **Link:** "The ranking debate therefore turns on which skill the degree is meant to certify." *Reason: closes the paragraph and hands over to the next.*

**Final answer:** the paragraph above — claim (fairer, measures learning), mechanism (recoverable weeks), counter-claim (rewards diligence), rebuttal (design flaw, not inherent limit), link (which skill is being certified).

**Why this works:** the counter-claim is stated at full strength, so the rebuttal does real work; the concession keeps the writer credible; and the claim is *narrowed* rather than defended absolutely, which is exactly what a top-band answer does.

⚠️ **Common trap:** The "straw man" counter-claim — inventing a weak objection ("some people say it is too modern") and knocking it down. Examiners and reviewers recognise it instantly. Second trap: a conclusion that introduces a brand-new argument, which cannot be developed and simply looks like an unfinished essay.

## 4.7 Editing, proofreading, referencing and academic integrity

### Intuition

**Editing** is changing what the text says; **proofreading** is checking that it says it correctly. They are different passes and must be done separately — if you proofread while editing, you polish sentences you are about to delete.

Think of a carpenter: editing is deciding the shape of the table, proofreading is sanding it. Nobody sands before cutting.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Edit in three passes, from big to small.** Pass 1: does each paragraph have one claim, and does the order work? Pass 2: are sentences clear, is anything repeated, is anything missing? Pass 3: grammar, spelling, punctuation, formatting. *Then* proofread aloud or backwards (read the last sentence first — it breaks the brain's auto-correction and exposes missing words).

**Step 2 — Run a mechanical proofreading checklist.** Use the same list every time; checklists catch what attention misses.

```text
[ ] Subject-verb agreement            : "the results shows" -> "shows"?
[ ] Tense consistency                 : one tense per section, past for method
[ ] Articles a / an / the             : countable singular nouns need one
[ ] Plurals of uncountables           : information, research, equipment, software (no -s)
[ ] Prepositions                      : discuss (no "about"), married to, depends on, consists of
[ ] Sentence completeness             : no fragments, no run-ons
[ ] Comma splices                     : "The test failed, we fixed it." -> full stop or semicolon
[ ] Apostrophes                       : its / it's, students / student's / students'
[ ] Number and unit spacing           : 5 V, 60 °C, 10 ms, ₹1,200 (consistent style)
[ ] Figure and table references       : every figure mentioned in the text, in order
[ ] Spell-check names and technical terms, then read them yourself (spell-check passes "form" for "from")
```

**Step 3 — Fix the errors Indian-English writing makes most often.** These are the ones that cost marks in an English paper while being invisible to the writer.

| Error type | Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Article omitted | "I am student of second year." | "I am a second-year student." | singular countable nouns need an article |
| Article added | "He gave me an advice." | "He gave me some advice." | advice is uncountable |
| Preposition | "We discussed about the results." | "We discussed the results." | *discuss* takes a direct object |
| Preposition | "This is different than mine." | "This is different from mine." | standard British/Indian academic usage |
| Countability | "one of my friend" | "one of my friends" | *one of* + plural |
| Plural of uncountable | "informations", "equipments", "softwares" | information, equipment, software | uncountable in standard English |
| Tense shift | "The sample was heated and then we observe the change." | "…and then the change was observed." | keep the tense of the section |
| since / for | "I am studying here since two years." | "I have been studying here for two years." | *since* + point in time, *for* + duration |
| less / fewer | "less students" | "fewer students" | *fewer* with countable plurals |
| "myself" as subject | "Myself Ravi Kumar." | "I am Ravi Kumar." | *myself* is reflexive, never a subject |
| Redundancy | "return back", "repeat again", "discuss about it" | return, repeat, discuss it | the second word adds nothing |

**Step 4 — Cite in a consistent style.** Two that you will meet constantly: **author–date** (APA/Harvard) — in text `(Sharma, 2021, p. 44)`, reference list alphabetical by author; and **numbered** (IEEE, standard in engineering) — in text `[1]`, reference list numbered in order of first appearance. Whichever you use, every in-text citation must have a reference entry and every entry must be cited. A reference list that includes sources you never cited is a marker's fastest signal that the list was copied.

**Step 5 — Paraphrase properly, not decoratively.** Real paraphrase changes the *structure* of the idea and keeps the *meaning*, with a citation. Copying with synonyms is not paraphrase.

```text
Original : "Because hydrometallurgical recovery costs more than newly mined metal, most spent
            batteries are stockpiled rather than recycled."
Copying  : "Since hydrometallurgical recovery is more expensive than newly mined metal, the
            majority of used batteries are stored instead of being recycled."   <- still copying
Paraphrase: "Economics, not technology, keeps used batteries in storage: recovery costs exceed
            the price of mined metal (Sharma, 2021, p. 44)."                    <- restructured
```

**Step 6 — Treat academic integrity as a design constraint, not a risk.** Keep your sources in a file as you read (not at the end), never paste a paragraph you have not paraphrased, and if you use an AI tool for anything beyond grammar, say so if your institution asks. The safe rule: **if you could not explain the sentence to a friend without the source open, it is not yours yet.**

**Example question:** Proofread and correct this paragraph, listing each error type: "Inspite of many research has been done on this topic, the results is not consistent. We discussed about the limitations in section 4. One of my friend helped me to collect the data, and we didn't faced any problem except the equipments was old."

**Solution:**

1. **"Inspite"** → "In spite" (two words). *Error type: spelling.*
2. **"many research has been done"** → "although much research has been done" / "despite the large body of research". *Error type: countability (research is uncountable) plus a broken concessive structure — "in spite of" cannot be followed by a clause.*
3. **"the results is"** → "the results are". *Error type: subject–verb agreement.*
4. **"discussed about"** → "discussed". *Error type: preposition after a transitive verb.*
5. **"One of my friend"** → "One of my friends". *Error type: `one of` + plural noun.*
6. **"didn't faced"** → "did not face". *Error type: auxiliary + base form; also contraction removed for formal register.*
7. **"the equipments was old"** → "the equipment was old". *Error type: uncountable noun (no plural) — and with the correction, the singular verb is right.*

Corrected version: "Although much research has been done on this topic, the results are not consistent. The limitations are discussed in Section 4. One of my friends helped me collect the data, and we did not face any problem except that the equipment was old."

**Final answer:** seven corrections — spelling (`In spite`), countability (`much research`), agreement (`results are`), preposition (`discussed`), `one of my friends`, `did not face`, `the equipment was`. The corrected paragraph is above.

**Why this works:** every fix is named by *type*, which is the only way proofreading improves: fixing "equipments" teaches nothing, but knowing "uncountable nouns take no plural" catches *informations*, *softwares* and *advices* in the same session.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Relying on a spell-checker and a grammar tool as your proofreading. They miss real-word errors ("form" for "from", "loose" for "lose", "then" for "than") and they cannot see that your Section 4 reference points to a section you deleted. The other trap: a reference list full of sources you never opened — which is both a plagiarism risk and an obvious tell.

## Solved examples

**Example 1 (easy) — thesis statement.** Turn "Write about electric vehicles in India" into a thesis.

Solution: (1) Pick a claim, not a topic: something must be arguable. (2) Add the scope (which vehicles, which market) and the condition that makes it true. (3) Check the test: could a reasonable person disagree? Model: "Electric two-wheelers, not cars, are the realistic route to cutting urban transport emissions in India, because they need neither new highway charging infrastructure nor a change in household parking habits." *Why it works:* it names the object (two-wheelers), the claim (the realistic route), and the mechanism (no new infrastructure), so the essay has somewhere to go.

**Example 2 (medium) — results paragraph.** Write a Results paragraph (about 80 words) from this data: tensile tests on three filament samples, 5 trials each; mean strength 48.2 MPa, 51.7 MPa, 44.9 MPa; standard deviations 1.9, 2.4, 6.1; one sample failed at 33.4 MPa.

Solution: (1) Report, do not interpret — save meaning for the discussion. (2) Lead with the comparison, then the spread, because the spread is the interesting part. (3) Name the outlier with its trial. Model: "Mean tensile strength was 51.7 MPa for Sample B, 48.2 MPa for Sample A and 44.9 MPa for Sample C, averaged over five trials each (Table 2). Variability differed markedly: standard deviations were 2.4 MPa, 1.9 MPa and 6.1 MPa respectively. Sample C also produced the single lowest reading, 33.4 MPa in trial 4, which lies 1.9 standard deviations below its own mean. Samples A and B were therefore consistent within 5% of their means, while Sample C was not." *Why it works:* no sentence says "this is because" — the interpretation is left for the discussion, which is what a marker checks.

**Example 3 (exam level) — argumentative conclusion plus rebuttal.** Write the final two paragraphs (about 150 words) of an essay arguing that coding should be taught in every school.

Solution: (1) State the strongest objection fairly. (2) Rebut by narrowing, not by denying. (3) Close by restating the position in new words with no new evidence. Model body paragraph: "The strongest objection is one of opportunity cost: every hour spent on syntax is an hour not spent on mathematics or a second language, and most schoolchildren will not become programmers. This objection has force, but it assumes coding means vocational training in a specific language. Taught as computational thinking — decomposing a problem, testing an assumption, debugging an error — it is closer to logic than to typing, and the transfer to mathematics is measurable rather than theoretical. What the objection correctly rules out is teaching a framework that will be obsolete in five years; it does not rule out teaching the reasoning that outlives it." Model conclusion: "Coding therefore belongs in the school curriculum not because every child will write software, but because every child will have to reason about systems that software controls. The curriculum should teach the thinking, and treat the language as replaceable." *Why it works:* the objection is stated at full strength, the rebuttal concedes a real limit (no frameworks), and the conclusion re-frames rather than repeats.

## Common traps and mistakes

- **A topic instead of a thesis.** "This essay is about AI in education" gives the reader nothing to agree or disagree with. *Cost: middle-band marks no matter how good the grammar is.*
- **Explanation skipped in PEEL.** Point + Evidence + Link with no explanation is a quotation, not an argument. *Cost: the paragraph proves nothing, so it earns nothing.*
- **Results mixed with discussion.** Interpretation inside the results table's paragraph makes the report look biased. *Cost: lost marks for structure, and your numbers become arguable.*
- **An overview that the data does not support.** Claiming "the gap narrowed" without subtracting the two figures. *Cost: the single most visible error in a data-description task.*
- **Percent vs percentage points.** "Rose by 59%" and "rose by 59 percentage points" are different claims about the same data. *Cost: a factually wrong sentence.*
- **Decorative connectives.** "Moreover, furthermore, in addition" where nothing is being added. *Cost: cohesion marks drop for over-linking.*
- **Passive used to hide responsibility.** "Mistakes were made." *Cost: reads as evasion, which is worse than the mistake.*
- **Pseudo-formal vocabulary.** "Utilise", "prior to", "in order to" for no reason. *Cost: longer text, no gain in formality, more errors.*
- **Straw-man counter-claims.** Refuting an objection nobody makes. *Cost: examiners recognise it and discount the whole paragraph.*
- **Proofreading with a tool only.** Spell-check passes "form" for "from" and cannot see a broken cross-reference. *Cost: careless-looking errors in an otherwise good document.*
- **Reference list padding.** Sources cited nowhere in the text. *Cost: looks like copied formatting and invites an integrity question.*

## Quick revision box

- ✅ **Thesis = arguable claim + scope + condition** (if nobody could disagree, it is not a thesis), and every body paragraph follows **PEEL: Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link** — the Explanation is where the marks live.
- ✅ **Report looks back, proposal looks forward.** Report: results + discussion + limitations. Proposal: objectives + method + timeline + budget.
- ✅ **Email: subject with the action, ask in the first two lines, deadline, attachment named.**
- ✅ **Chart: introduce → overview → grouped details.** Compute the gap and the multiples before you write them; use *percentage points* for differences.
- ✅ **Coherence = route, cohesion = couplings.** Add a connective only where the logic turns.
- ✅ **Formal = impersonal + quantified + hedged**, not long words. Passive when the actor is irrelevant, never to hide who acted.
- ✅ **Argument = claim + counter-claim + rebuttal**, where the rebuttal concedes, then narrows.
- ✅ **Edit big to small, then proofread with a checklist** (articles, prepositions, plurals, agreement, tense). **Paraphrase restructures** — copying with synonyms is still copying — and you cite only sources you actually opened.

## Practice questions

1. *(easy)* Rewrite formally and objectively: "I think our results are pretty good, but the lab was very noisy so maybe some readings are wrong."
2. *(easy)* Write a subject line and the first two lines of an email asking your professor for a two-day extension on a report, giving one concrete reason.
3. *(medium)* Write a PEEL paragraph (about 110 words) on: "Should attendance be compulsory in first-year engineering classes?"
4. *(medium)* A chart shows average time spent on three activities (study, social media, sleep) for first-year students in 2019 and 2024: study 3.2 → 2.8 h, social media 2.1 → 3.4 h, sleep 7.1 → 6.4 h. Write a 90-word description with an overview first.
5. *(medium-hard)* Write a memo (about 100 words) from a lab in-charge to all second-year students about a change in lab timings, with the action required stated once, clearly.
6. *(exam level)* Write the counter-claim and rebuttal paragraphs (about 150 words) for: "Social media does more harm than good to teenagers."

## Answers

1. Model: "The results are broadly consistent with the expected values, although the acoustic noise level in the laboratory may have introduced measurement error in some readings." *Key step:* remove "I think" and "maybe", quantify or name the error source, keep the hedge on the *error*, not on your opinion. (Naming the noise level in dB would be better still.)
2. Model: Subject — "Extension request: Unit 2 report, 2 days (Ravi Kumar, 22CS1043)". Body — "I am requesting a two-day extension for the Unit 2 report, to Friday 21 November. The circuit simulation files were lost when the lab server was reset on Monday and the results must be re-run." *Key step:* the ask and the deadline are in the first two lines, and the reason is a fact, not a feeling.
3. Model: "Compulsory attendance improves outcomes mainly for the students who would otherwise attend least, which is the opposite of how it is usually justified. In a first-year cohort of 120, the students with the lowest attendance were also those with the weakest school mathematics background, and it was this group whose marks rose most when attendance was enforced; strong students gained little. The mechanism is not discipline but exposure: a lecture demonstrates the *sequence* of a derivation, which a slide deck flattens into a result. What compulsory attendance cannot fix is a badly taught class, where presence produces no learning and resentment instead." *Key step:* one claim, one piece of evidence with a mechanism, and a concession at the end that keeps the paragraph honest.
4. Model: "The table compares the average daily hours first-year students spent on study, social media and sleep in 2019 and 2024. Overall, time shifted from study and sleep towards social media, and the largest change was a 1.3-hour rise in social media use. Study time fell by 0.4 hours, from 3.2 to 2.8, while sleep dropped 0.7 hours to 6.4. Social media was the only activity to increase, roughly 62%, and by 2024 it exceeded study time by 0.6 hours, whereas in 2019 it had been 1.1 hours lower." *Key step:* compute the differences first (1.3, 0.4, 0.7), state them as hours or percentage points, and give one comparison that a reader could not see without arithmetic.
5. Model: "**TO:** All second-year students **FROM:** Lab In-charge, CS Laboratory **DATE:** 18 November 2026 **SUBJECT:** Change of lab timings from 1 December. From 1 December, the second-year DBMS lab moves from Thursday 2 pm to Tuesday 10 am, because the Thursday slot is required for the new networks batch. Students with a Tuesday class clash must email the lab in-charge by 25 November with their timetable; no changes will be made after that date. Attendance registers will follow the new slot from the first session." *Key step:* no salutation, action stated once with a hard deadline, reason given in one clause.
6. Model: "The strongest objection is that the evidence is correlational: teenagers who spend four hours a day on social platforms also report worse sleep and lower mood, but the causality may run the other way, with unhappy adolescents retreating to their screens. This objection is serious and partly correct, which is why the sweeping claim should be narrowed. Where randomised and longitudinal studies exist, the effect sizes are small and concentrated in girls and in appearance-focused platforms, not in social media as a whole. The defensible position is therefore not that social media harms teenagers but that it harms a minority substantially, and that the design features driving that harm — infinite scroll and public metrics — are identifiable and removable." *Key step:* concede the causal problem, then narrow the claim using the same evidence, so the rebuttal strengthens rather than abandons the argument.
