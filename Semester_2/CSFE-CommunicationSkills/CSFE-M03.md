## CO3 – Reading

*Friend-style notes: intuition first, then the rule, then a small example.*

## The big picture

Reading is the skill you will use most in four years of engineering, and almost nobody teaches it
properly. In school you read one way: start at the top, read every word, reach the bottom. In college
that method quietly fails, because the load is not just bigger — it is different in kind. In one week
you might read a 12-page textbook section, a 2-page lab manual, a 6-page research paper, a 40-page
datasheet, a question paper and thirty emails. Reading all of those the same way is like using a screwdriver for everything, including hammering nails.

Think of reading as a bicycle with three gears, plus a torch:

- **Top gear (skimming)** — fast, you see the shape of the road, you will miss small stones. Fine, because you only wanted to know where the road goes.
- **Radar (scanning)** — you are not travelling at all; you are hunting one specific thing: a date, a number, a name, an error code.
- **Low gear with a torch (intensive reading)** — slow, deliberate, every sentence examined, because each line matters: a proof, a clause, a method you must reproduce.
- **The torch also turns on your own understanding** — that is critical reading: "is this true, and who gains if I believe it?"

Why it matters for marks: most "I studied but scored less" cases in a communication paper are not
knowledge problems, they are reading problems — misreading what the question asked, missing the word
*not*, answering from general knowledge instead of from the passage, or not noticing that the passage never actually said the thing.

## Basics you must know first

- **Main idea** — the one sentence the paragraph exists to support; usually first or second sentence,
  sometimes the last.
- **Topic sentence** — the sentence that states a paragraph's main idea.
- **Inference** — a conclusion the text supports but never states ("the bus was 40 minutes late" implies irritation without anyone writing "I was irritated").
- **Register** — level of formality (a WhatsApp message vs a project report).
- **Genre** — type of text (textbook, paper, datasheet, blog, manual). Genre tells you where the
  useful parts usually sit.
- **Signpost words** — `however`, `therefore`, `for example`, `in contrast`: the road signs of a
  paragraph, telling you the writer is turning, proving or concluding.
- **Schema** — your background knowledge of the topic. More schema, faster reading, because you
  predict correctly instead of decoding every word.

## 3.1 Skimming, scanning and intensive reading

### Intuition

You walk into a large library to find material on drones. Three jobs, three behaviours. (1) You read
only the shelf labels down each aisle — 30 seconds, and you know which two aisles matter. That is
**skimming**: fast, wide, structural. (2) You open one book's index and hunt the single word
"payload" — you are not reading the book, you are hunting a target. That is **scanning**. (3) You sit
with the one chapter that matters and read every line with a pen, because you must explain it in a seminar tomorrow. That is **intensive reading**.

Same library, same student, three modes. Nobody tells you to switch; you just do. The skill is switching *on purpose*, and knowing which mode the task needs.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Skim the skeleton, not the flesh.** Read the title, headings, first sentence of each
paragraph, the last paragraph, and any bold or boxed text. Ignore examples, numbers and repeated
explanations. In 3 minutes you should be able to say what the text is about, how it is organised, and
whether it deserves a proper read. *Tiny example:* headings read *Introduction / Causes of packet loss
/ Measuring loss / Mitigation / Conclusion* tell you the page runs problem → measurement → solution, so you can jump straight to "Mitigation" later.

**Step 2 — Scan only with a named target.** Say the target to yourself first ("operating temperature
range"), then move your eyes down the page looking only for the *shape* of the answer — digits with a
degree symbol, a capitalised name, a date. Do not read sentences. *Tiny example:* scanning for
"3.3 V" you catch digit patterns and fly past "intended for low-voltage applications", which is correct behaviour, because it contains no digits.

**Step 3 — Read intensively with a question in hand.** For each paragraph ask: what is the claim,
what supports it, how does it link to the last one? Underline the claim, box the support, write a
5-word margin note. A page should end with 4–5 margin notes; none means you were looking at words,
not reading. *Tiny example:* "Accuracy improved by 4%, but only for inputs shorter than 50 tokens" —
the second half is where the marks live, so this sentence gets intensive treatment.

**Step 4 — Choose the mode from the task, not from the text.** The same paper is skimmed to decide
whether to cite it, scanned to find its sample size, and read intensively to reproduce its method.

| Mode | Typical speed | Depth | Eyes do | Use when | Typical task |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Skimming | ~400–800 wpm | 40–60%, the shape | headings, first lines, last para | deciding relevance, previewing | "does this support my claim?" |
| Scanning | fastest, not measured in wpm | near zero for sentences | hunt one target string | you know the exact fact you want | "find the 2022 figure" |
| Intensive | ~150–250 wpm | 80–100% | every sentence, with a pen | each line's meaning matters | "explain this method" |
| Extensive (bonus) | comfortable | enough to enjoy | flowing, no pen | building vocabulary over months | a novel, popular science |

Those speeds are typical adult ranges, not rules. What matters is the *ratio*: skimming should feel at least twice as fast as intensive reading.

**Example question:** You are handed a 6-page project report on solar-powered water pumps. You have 8
minutes to decide whether it is useful for your project, then you must find the exact efficiency
figure reported for the pump used in the field trial. Which mode for which step, and in what order?

**Solution:**

1. **Minutes 0–5: skim.** Title, abstract, all headings, first sentence per section, conclusion.
   *Reason: the skeleton tells you the scope (design? field trial? costing?) and whether it matches your project.*
2. **Minute 5–6: write the decision.** One line: "Field trial of a 1 hp solar pump in Rajasthan;
   reports efficiency and cost; relevant to my costing section." *Reason: an unwritten decision means you re-read the same report next week.*
3. **Minutes 6–8: scan.** Go to Results and hunt only a percentage pattern near the word
   "efficiency"; do not read the surrounding prose. *Reason: relevance is settled, so you need one number, and scanning is the only mode that finds one number fast.*
4. **Minute 8+: intensive, but only 3–4 sentences around that number** — the conditions (irradiance,
   head, flow rate). *Reason: an efficiency figure without its conditions is unusable and is exactly what gets marked wrong in a lab report.*

**Final answer:** skim (relevance) → scan (locate the figure) → intensive on 3–4 sentences around it
(capture the conditions). Order matters: skimming before scanning stops you hunting a number inside an irrelevant report.

**Why this works:** each mode answers a different *type* of question — "what is this?" (skim), "where
is X?" (scan), "what exactly does X mean here?" (intensive). Mixing them is the classic time-waster:
students scan a report they never skimmed, so they do not know which section to scan.

⚠️ **Common trap:** "Skimming" is not "reading fast". Reading every sentence quickly is just bad
intensive reading, and you remember nothing. Skimming means deliberately *skipping*. And scanning is
not skimming slower — if you catch yourself understanding whole sentences while scanning, you have lost the target.

## 3.2 Reading speed and comprehension

### Intuition

Two students read the same 3-page handout. One finishes in 12 minutes and remembers nothing; the other
finishes in 6 minutes and can explain it. The difference is not intelligence or eye speed — it is that
the second student has stopped three habits that silently eat time. Think of reading as a pipeline with three leaks:

- **Leak 1, your mouth.** You silently "say" each word. Your eyes can take in 4–5 words at a glance,
  but your inner voice manages about one word per quarter-second, so your eyes wait for your mouth. That is **sub-vocalisation**.
- **Leak 2, your rewinding.** You finish a line, doubt yourself, jump back two lines. That is
  **regression**, and it usually means you were not attending, not that you missed something.
- **Leak 3, word-by-word reading.** "the / quick / brown / fox" instead of "the quick brown fox" as one chunk — the absence of **chunking**.

Fix the leaks and speed rises with no loss of understanding. That last part is the whole point: speed without comprehension is not reading, it is page-turning.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Measure a baseline honestly.** Pick a text at your level, get its word count (a word processor shows one), read once at normal pace for understanding, time it.

```text
WPM = words read / minutes taken
Example: 900 words in 4 min 30 s -> 4.5 min -> 900 / 4.5 = 200 wpm

Comprehension % = (correct answers / total questions) x 100
Example: 6 correct out of 8 -> (6 / 8) x 100 = 75%
```

Use questions you did not write yourself. Typical adult silent reading of academic English is roughly 200–250 wpm at 70–80% comprehension — landmarks, not targets.

**Step 2 — Combine both numbers into one honest score.**

```text
Effective reading rate = WPM x comprehension fraction
Example: 200 wpm at 75% -> 200 x 0.75 = 150 effective wpm
```

This single number is what you actually try to improve, because it cannot be gamed.

**Step 3 — Kill sub-vocalisation mechanically, not by willpower.** Two tricks that work by occupying
the inner voice: hold a pencil between your teeth, or silently count "one, two, three" while reading.
Both feel strange for a week. The goal is not zero inner voice — for hard technical text you want some — it is to stop it being the bottleneck on easy text.

**Step 4 — Chunk: three or four eye stops per line, not ten.**

```text
Word by word : The / compiler / reports / an / error / when / the / semicolon / is / missing.
Chunked      : The compiler reports / an error when / the semicolon is missing / at the line end.
```

Four stops instead of ten, identical meaning. Practise by slash-marking a paragraph, then re-reading it once per group.

**Step 5 — Cut regressions with a purpose.** Regression is mostly a symptom of passive reading. Give
yourself one question before you start ("what does this section claim about latency?") and your eyes
stop wandering. If you truly must re-read, do it after the paragraph, not mid-sentence.

**Step 6 — Respect the tradeoff.** Push 20–30% above your comfort band and comprehension holds; push
100% above it and comprehension collapses, you re-read, and total time goes *up*. Find the fastest
speed that keeps comprehension above roughly 70% *for that genre*. A datasheet tolerates a very fast scan because you only want numbers; a proof tolerates no speed at all.

| Leak | Looks like | Why it costs you | Fix | Time to effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sub-vocalisation | lips or throat move, you "hear" words | caps speed near speaking rate | pencil trick, silent counting | 1–2 weeks |
| Regression | eyes jump back mid-sentence | same words read 2–3 times | purpose per paragraph | 1 week |
| No chunking | one fixation per word | 3–4x too many eye stops | slash-marked phrases | 2–3 weeks |
| Passive reading | eyes move, mind drifts | comprehension under 50%, so speed is wasted | margin notes, a question first | immediate |

**Example question:** You read a 600-word passage in 3 minutes and answer 6 of 8 comprehension
questions correctly. On a similar passage you push harder: 600 words in 2 minutes, but only 5 of 8 correct. Which read was actually more efficient, and what should you change?

**Solution:**

1. **Passage 1 speed:** `600 / 3 = 200 wpm`. *Reason: WPM is words divided by minutes.*
2. **Passage 1 comprehension:** `(6 / 8) x 100 = 75%`. *Reason: correct over total, as a percentage.*
3. **Passage 1 effective rate:** `200 x 0.75 = 150 effective wpm`. *Reason: multiplying stops a fast-but-wrong read from looking good.*
4. **Passage 2 speed:** `600 / 2 = 300 wpm`; **comprehension** `(5 / 8) x 100 = 62.5%`;
   **effective rate** `300 x 0.625 = 187.5 effective wpm`. *Reason: same formula, so the two reads are comparable.*
5. **Compare:** 187.5 > 150, so the faster read was genuinely more efficient — but 62.5% is below the ~70% floor, which means comprehension is now the risk, not speed.
6. **What to change:** hold around 250–270 wpm on this genre and recover the lost marks by scanning
   back for the two guessed answers. *Reason: you want the highest effective rate that still clears 70%.*

**Final answer:** Passage 1 = 200 wpm at 75% = **150 effective wpm**; Passage 2 = 300 wpm at 62.5% =
**187.5 effective wpm**. Passage 2 wins on efficiency, but its comprehension has dropped below the
safe band, so settle near 250–270 wpm and verify guessed answers by scanning rather than pushing speed further.

**Why this works:** it replaces the vague feeling "I read faster now" with numbers you can track
weekly, and it blocks the common self-deception where speed gains are really comprehension losses.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Treating wpm as a score. A student at 400 wpm and 50% comprehension is *slower in
real terms* than one at 200 wpm and 85%, because the first must read everything twice. Never quote a speed without its comprehension figure.

## 3.3 Technical and academic texts

### Intuition

A research paper is not written in the order you should read it. The author writes Introduction →
Method → Results → Discussion, because that is the logic of an argument; a reader with 20 minutes
should read almost backwards. It is like arriving at a railway station: you look for the platform
board and the exit signs first, not the architect's drawings. A **datasheet** is a reference table
with prose sprinkled on it, so you scan it like a dictionary. A **lab manual** is a recipe: read it before you start, never during.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Know the five rooms of a paper.** Abstract = what was done and found (80% of your citation
decision). Introduction = why it matters and what is missing (the gap is in its *last* paragraph).
Method = how it was done. Results = what the numbers show. Discussion/Conclusion = what it means, plus limits and future work.

**Step 2 — Read a paper in 20 minutes in three passes.** A widely used approach, and the timings below are a workable version of it:

| Minutes | Pass | You do | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | reconnaissance | title, abstract, keywords, all headings, last para of conclusion | one line: what is this about? |
| 2–6 | skeleton | every figure and table caption, last para of introduction | one line: claimed contribution? |
| 6–14 | evidence | results figures properly; method only where you doubt something | three notes: numbers, method, limitation |
| 14–17 | trust check | sample size, baselines, assumptions, who funded it | one line: do I believe it? |
| 17–20 | write and decide | 4-line summary in your own words | note card filed: keep / discard |

Most papers do not survive this, and that is the point — 20 minutes, not three hours. If one survives and matters, *then* read it intensively.

**Step 3 — Read a datasheet by columns.** Everything sits under **Min / Typ / Max**. `Typ` means
"typically measured" — it is *not* a guarantee; only `Min` and `Max` are guaranteed, and only over
the stated test conditions. So: read **Absolute Maximum Ratings** first (what destroys the part),
then **Electrical Characteristics** (what you may design with) with its conditions row, then the pin description.

**Step 4 — Read a lab manual as a procedure with variables.** Before the lab, mark four things: the
aim, the independent variable (what you change), the dependent variable (what you measure), and the
precautions (a list that exists because a previous batch broke something). In the lab you only glance at the observation table.

**Example question:** You have 20 minutes to decide whether this paper is worth a full read for your
mini-project on reducing mobile-app battery drain. Give the pass-by-pass outputs and the decision.

> *Abstract (sample text):* "Background: background location tracking is a major source of battery
> drain in Android apps, yet developers rarely measure its cost. Method: we instrumented 40 popular
> apps and logged location-request frequency over 14 days on 12 devices. Results: apps requesting
> location every 30 seconds consumed 11–17% more battery per hour than apps batching requests every
> 5 minutes, with no measurable loss in delivered accuracy. We propose a batching guideline and a
> linter that flags high-frequency requesters. Conclusion: batching is a cheap, general fix, though
> our sample covers only foreground location requests."

**Solution:**

1. **Pass 1 (0–2 min):** "Battery cost of frequent location requests in Android apps, measured on 40
   apps and 12 devices." *Reason: the abstract's first two sentences carry topic and scale.*
2. **Pass 2 (2–6 min):** two contributions — an empirical measurement (11–17% extra drain) and a tool
   plus guideline (batch every 5 minutes). *Reason: "we propose" marks the contribution, "we instrumented" marks the evidence.*
3. **Pass 3 (6–14 min):** check the baseline behind 11–17% (same apps with batching on?), then skim
   the method for one thing only — was battery measured by software estimate or hardware meter?
   *Reason: the number transfers to your project only if the measurement method is comparable.*
4. **Trust check (14–17 min):** 40 apps, 12 devices, 14 days is reasonable but narrow, and the
   authors themselves flag the foreground-only limit. *Reason: stated limits are the cheapest honest critique you will ever get.*
5. **Decide (17–20 min):** keep, read Method and Results fully later. Card: "11–17% drain, 30 s polling vs 5 min batching; 40 apps / 12 devices / 14 days; limit: foreground only."

**Final answer:** **keep, read fully later.** The 20 minutes already produced the two things you need
— a number to compare against and a method to borrow — plus the authors' own limitation.

**Why this works:** it never reads the paper in the order it was written, and it ends in a *decision plus a note*, the only output that survives the week.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Quoting `Typ` as a guaranteed value, or quoting a headline percentage without its
test conditions — both give numbers that are correct on the page and wrong in your report. Second
trap: reading Method first because it "feels like the real content"; you cannot judge which details matter until you know the claim.

## 3.4 Vocabulary in context

### Intuition

Someone at a wedding says "he is my mother's brother's son". You have never heard his name, yet you
know exactly who he is — from the *structure* of the description. Words work the same way. You rarely
need a dictionary; you need to look at what surrounds the word. Two sources of clues: **inside the
word** (morphology: `un-` + `predict` + `-able` = not able to be predicted) and **around the word**
(context: the sentence often defines, contrasts, exemplifies or causes the word, if you know where to look).

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Split prefix + root + suffix.**

```text
in + comprehens + ible  -> not able to be comprehended = impossible to understand
hyper + tension         -> tension above normal
mono + lith + ic        -> "one stone" -> cut from a single block
```

**Step 2 — Learn the four context clue types.**

| Clue | Signal words | Example | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | `is`, `means`, `that is`, `:`, `—` | "The cache is *volatile*: it loses its contents when power is cut." | loses data without power |
| Contrast | `but`, `unlike`, `however`, `rather than` | "Unlike the earlier *brittle* design, the new one tolerates a node failure." | brittle = fails completely under stress |
| Example | `such as`, `for example`, `including` | "Ephemeral data, such as a session token, is discarded at logout." | ephemeral = short-lived |
| Cause–effect | `because`, `therefore`, `so`, `as a result` | "The system is *stateless*; therefore any server can serve the next request." | keeps no memory of past requests |

**Step 3 — Keep a prefix/suffix toolbox in your head.**

| Prefix | Meaning | Example | Suffix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| un-, in-, non- | not | unsafe | -able, -ible | can be | portable |
| re- | again | rebuild | -less | without | stateless |
| pre-, post- | before, after | preprocess | -ous, -ive | having the quality | porous, adaptive |
| sub-, super- | under, above | subset | -fy, -ise/-ize | to make | simplify |
| inter-, intra- | between, within | interconnect | -tion, -ment | act or result of | allocation |
| mono-, multi- | one, many | multithreaded | -ity | state of being | scalability |
| micro-, macro- | small, large | microservice | -logy, -graphy | study, writing | topology |
| anti-, co- | against, together | co-processor | -meter, -scope | measure, view | thermometer |

**Step 4 — Watch false friends and Indian-English habits.** A **false friend** looks familiar but
means something else in standard English. `actually` = "in fact", **not** "currently" ("Actually I am
in second year" should be "I am currently in second year"). `eventually` = "in the end, after a long
time", not "possibly". `doubt` = uncertainty, not a question — "I have a doubt" is understood in
India, but write "I have a question" for an international reader. `revert` (to return to a previous
state) is used for "reply" only in Indian English; `prepone` is an Indian coinage (standard: "bring
forward"); `out of station` → "out of town"; `kindly do the needful` → "please take the necessary
action". None of these are crimes — they mark a variety of English — but in formal academic writing and international exams the standard form is safer.

**Example question:** Using only the sentences, work out what *idempotent* and *amortised* mean and name the clue type used for each.

> "A good retry mechanism needs an *idempotent* operation: if the request is sent twice, the effect
> on the database must be the same as sending it once. This is why the team rejected the naive
> 'increment counter' endpoint, whose cost was *amortised* over thousands of retries because each
> retry silently doubled the count."

**Solution:**

1. **Find the definition of *idempotent*.** The colon introduces the explanation, and "must be the
   same as sending it once" *is* the definition. Clue: **definition (punctuation)**. *Reason: a colon after a term almost always announces "here is what it means".*
2. **Paraphrase immediately:** doing it twice has the same result as doing it once. *Reason: a word left unparaphrased stays a label with nothing behind it.*
3. **Attack *amortised* by morphology:** `a-` (to) + `mort` (death, Latin) + `-ise` (to make) — to
   bring to death, i.e. to pay off or spread out until gone. *Reason: the root gives a direction.*
4. **Confirm with context:** "cost was amortised over thousands of retries because each retry doubled
   the count" — cost spread across many retries. Clue: **cause–effect (`because`)**. *Reason: two independent clues agreeing is a strong inference.*
5. **Substitute back:** "the cost was spread out over thousands of retries" fits without contradiction. *Reason: substitution is the only real test of a guess.*

**Final answer:** *idempotent* = an operation whose repeated application gives the same result as one
application (definition clue, colon). *amortised* = a cost spread out or paid off gradually across many repetitions (morphology `a- + mort + -ise`, confirmed by `because`).

**Why this works:** it requires two independent evidence sources before committing to a meaning, then tests the guess by substitution — so the answer is checkable, not a feeling.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Guessing from the *sound* of a word rather than its parts — `inflammable` means
"easily set on fire", not "not flammable". And one clue is not proof: if the definition clue and the
contrast clue disagree, re-read the whole paragraph before writing a meaning down.

## 3.5 Critical reading

### Intuition

A shopkeeper says "this phone has the best camera in the market". You do not write that in your
project report. You ask: best according to whom, tested how, and does he earn more if I buy this one?
That reflex — *who says so, on what evidence, and what do they gain* — is critical reading. It is not
being negative; it is being an engineer instead of a photocopier. Critical reading separates four
things ordinary reading blends: **claim** (what the author wants you to believe), **evidence** (the
data or citations offered), **assumption** (the unstated belief that must hold), and **bias** (the interest that shaped which evidence was chosen).

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — State the claim in one sentence:** "The author claims that ___." If you cannot fill the blank, you have not found it yet.

**Step 2 — Separate claim from evidence.** Evidence looks like numbers, sample sizes, controlled
comparisons, named studies, sourced quotations. Non-evidence: "obviously", "everyone knows", "studies show" (which studies?), anecdotes, authority without data.

**Step 3 — Name the assumption.** Test: if it were false, would the claim collapse? Claim "our app
cut study time by 30%", evidence "users reported studying less" — the assumption is that self-reported
time is accurate *and* that less time means the same learning. Both are shaky, and that is where a good critique lives.

**Step 4 — Check bias and framing.** Who paid? What is measured, and what is conveniently *not*
measured? Which comparison was chosen ("compared to our older version" is a much easier baseline than
"compared to the market leader")? Watch loaded words: *revolutionary*, *seamless*, *industry-leading* are marketing, not evidence.

**Step 5 — Run the five-point source check.**

| Check | Question | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | who wrote it, with what credentials? | no author, no affiliation |
| Currency | when was it published? | undated page, or old for a fast field |
| Accuracy | are claims backed by checkable data? | round numbers, no method, no sample size |
| Purpose | informing, selling or persuading? | a company blog praising its own product |
| Audience | who is it written for? | simplified for the public, quoted as research |

**Example question:** A company blog says: "AI will replace 40% of software engineering jobs by 2030
— our survey of 500 developers proves it." Write a four-part critical evaluation (claim, evidence, assumption, verdict).

**Solution:**

1. **Claim:** "40% of software engineering jobs will disappear by 2030 because of AI." *Reason: strip the persuasive wording before judging anything.*
2. **Evidence:** a survey of 500 developers — but a survey of developers' *opinions* is not evidence
   about *job numbers*; nothing described counts jobs. *Reason: match the evidence type to the claim type; opinions cannot support a headcount forecast.*
3. **Assumption:** that developers' predictions are accurate forecasts, and that 500 self-selected
   respondents represent the global profession. *Reason: the claim collapses if either fails.*
4. **Bias and purpose:** the author sells AI tooling, so a dramatic forecast serves the business, and
   "proves it" pre-empts doubt. *Reason: purpose explains the choice of evidence and the certainty of tone.*
5. **Verdict:** unreliable as evidence; usable at most as an illustration of industry anxiety, and
   only if labelled as opinion. *Reason: a critique must end in a decision — cite it or do not.*

**Final answer:** claim = 40% job loss by 2030; evidence = a 500-developer opinion survey, which
cannot support a headcount forecast; assumption = developer predictions are accurate and the sample is representative; verdict = **not citable as evidence of job loss**.

**Why this works:** it keeps the three levels apart — what is said, what is shown, what is smuggled in — and converts criticism into a decision instead of a complaint.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Confusing "I disagree with this" with "this is badly evidenced". A paper whose
conclusion you dislike can be methodologically excellent, and one you agree with can be junk. Attack the link between evidence and claim, not the claim's politics.

## 3.6 Proficiency-test reading tasks

### Intuition

IELTS, TOEFL and PTE do not test whether you understood the passage; they test whether you can
*prove* a specific claim about it, in a fixed format, under time pressure. That is a different game,
and it has known tricks: every task type has one signature trap, and once you know it you stop losing
the same two marks every attempt. Formats change over the years — IELTS Academic Reading is currently
3 passages / 40 questions / 60 minutes, and the shorter TOEFL iBT format uses 2 passages of 10
questions in about 35 minutes — so confirm the current pattern on the official site; treat any pattern printed in a coaching book as a claim to check.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Read the questions before the passage** (for most task types). Knowing that question 4 is about the year 2019 turns the passage into a scanning exercise.

**Step 2 — Learn the signature trap of each type.**

| Task type | Asks | Signature trap | Rule that saves you |
|---|---|---|---|
| True / False / Not Given | agree, disagree, or silent? | treating Not Given as False | FALSE needs the passage to *contradict*; no contradicting words means NOT GIVEN |
| Yes / No / Not Given | same, about the writer's *views* | confusing fact with opinion | Y/N/NG is opinion, T/F/NG is fact |
| Matching headings | one heading per paragraph | matching a heading to a word that appears there | the heading must cover the main idea, not a detail |
| Summary completion | fill gaps from the passage | copying words when the gap needs a grammar change | the finished sentence must be grammatical *and* meaning-identical |
| Matching information | which paragraph mentions X | answering from memory | mark the exact line before answering |

**Step 3 — Use the pointer test for Not Given.** Put a finger on the sentence that decides the answer.
If your finger has nothing to rest on, it is NOT GIVEN. No exceptions, no "it is implied somewhere".

**Step 4 — Budget per passage, not per question.** For 60 minutes and 40 questions that is roughly 20
minutes a passage including answer transfer. If a question has eaten 90 seconds, guess, mark it, move.

**Example question:** Read the passage and answer (a) three True/False/Not Given statements, (b) the best heading, (c) the summary gaps.

> "Recycling of lithium-ion batteries has lagged far behind their production. Although collection
> schemes exist in the European Union, only about a fifth of spent batteries are currently recycled
> there. The main obstacle is economic rather than technical: hydrometallurgical processes can recover
> over 90% of lithium, cobalt and nickel, but the recovered material often costs more than newly mined
> metal. Several start-ups are therefore testing direct recycling, which preserves the cathode
> structure instead of dissolving it. Whether this approach can scale remains unclear."

> (a) i. Battery recycling has not kept pace with battery manufacturing. ii. Technical limits are the
> biggest barrier to recycling. iii. Direct recycling has been shown to work at industrial scale.
> (b) Headings: (i) Why recycling is uneconomic (ii) The chemistry of lithium extraction (iii) Battery
> demand in Europe
> (c) "Although over 90% of key metals can be recovered by ______ processes, the recovered material
> often costs more than ______ metal."

**Solution:**

1. **(a) i = TRUE.** "has lagged far behind their production" says the same thing as "has not kept pace". *Reason: same meaning, different words — that is what TRUE looks like.*
2. **(a) ii = FALSE.** The passage says the obstacle is "economic rather than technical". *Reason:
   FALSE needs an actual contradiction, and "rather than technical" contradicts the statement.*
3. **(a) iii = NOT GIVEN.** The passage says scaling "remains unclear" — neither confirmed nor denied.
   *Reason: the pointer test fails, and nothing contradicts it, so it cannot be FALSE either.*
4. **(b) = (i).** The spine is: recycling is rare → because recovered material costs more than mined metal. *Reason: (ii) covers one clause only and (iii) is never mentioned.*
5. **(c) gap 1 = "hydrometallurgical"** (the named process recovering over 90%); **gap 2 = "newly
   mined"** (copy the passage's phrase so the contrast survives). *Reason: grammatical fit plus identical meaning.*

**Final answer:** (a) TRUE, FALSE, NOT GIVEN; (b) heading (i); (c) hydrometallurgical; newly mined.

**Why this works:** every answer is anchored to a phrase you could underline, and the "no evidence" case was settled by the pointer test rather than by intuition.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Over-thinking T/F/NG. Students find a sentence that merely *hints* at the statement
and mark TRUE, or mark FALSE because "the passage doesn't say that" — which is the definition of NOT
GIVEN. Two checks: can I underline support? → TRUE. Can I underline a contradiction? → FALSE. Neither?
→ NOT GIVEN. Write answers exactly as instructed (`TRUE`, not `T` and not `True, because…`).

## 3.7 Research-based reading

### Intuition

You would not build a project by copying one repository blindly; you would compare several and see
where they agree. Literature reading is the same — the unit of work is not "the paper", it is **the
claim**, and your job is to find out what several independent sources say about that one claim. Three
habits make it fast: a **search strategy** (so you find the right ten papers, not the first ten),
**note cards** (so you never re-read a paper), and **synthesis** (so your paragraph shows a conversation, not a list).

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Search with a strategy.** Turn the topic into 2–4 keyword groups and combine with Boolean operators: `AND` narrows, `OR` widens with synonyms, `NOT` excludes.

```text
("flipped classroom" OR "inverted classroom") AND (engineering OR "technical education")
AND (India OR "Indian colleges")
```

Then filter by year and type, and **snowball**: open the best paper's reference list and go backwards;
open "cited by" and go forwards. Engineering databases commonly used: IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar.

**Step 2 — Fill a note card immediately, five fields.**

```text
SOURCE     : Menon & Rao (2022), journal, vol(issue), pages
CLAIM      : flipped classroom raised end-semester marks ~6% vs lecture-only
EVIDENCE   : 210 second-year students, one circuits course, one institution
LIMIT      : authors admit single-institution sample
MY COMMENT : good for the "marks" angle; too small for general claims
PAGE       : p. 214 (so the exact number can be cited later)
```

If you cannot fill CLAIM and EVIDENCE, you have not read it yet — do not file the card.

**Step 3 — Synthesise with three moves.** A synthesis paragraph **agrees and adds** ("both report a
small gain, and Y adds that it persists"), **extends** ("Z shows it holds in large classes"), or
**qualifies** ("however, Y warns that most studies measure marks only"). Never write "Author A says… Author B says… Author C says…" — that is a list, not a synthesis.

**Step 4 — Avoid plagiarism *while* reading.** Two rules: **close the source before you paraphrase**
(if the original is on screen your eyes copy its sentence structure even when you change the words),
and keep the page number, because you cannot cite a number you cannot locate. Quote only when exact
wording matters, keep quotes under roughly a tenth of your text, and mark them with quotation marks plus a citation.

**Example question:** Using these two note cards, write a three-sentence synthesis paragraph for a
report on flipped classrooms in Indian engineering colleges, with correct attribution.

> Card A — Menon & Rao (2022): 210 second-year students, one circuits course, one institution;
> flipped classroom raised end-semester marks by about 6% over lecture-only; authors caution that the
> sample is single-institution.
> Card B — Iyer (2023): review of 34 studies in Indian engineering colleges; effects positive but
> small (2–8%); most studies measure marks only, not lab or design skills.

**Solution:**

1. **Sentence 1, agreement:** "Both Menon and Rao (2022) and Iyer (2023) report that flipping an
   engineering classroom produces a measurable but modest improvement in student marks." *Reason: start with what the sources share; that is the finding you can state confidently.*
2. **Sentence 2, attributed figures:** "Menon and Rao found a gain of about 6% in a single circuits
   course, while Iyer's review of 34 studies places the range at 2–8%." *Reason: attach each figure to its source so the reader knows which one generalises.*
3. **Sentence 3, qualification that opens your contribution:** "Iyer notes, however, that almost all
   of this evidence rests on marks rather than lab or design skills, a gap this study addresses by
   measuring lab performance." *Reason: the limitation is where your own work enters; without it the paragraph is a summary, not a synthesis.*

**Final answer:** the three-sentence paragraph above — agreement, attributed figures, then a
qualification that opens your own contribution. (Author names and years are placeholders showing the format; use your own real sources.)

**Why this works:** every number carries its source, the sources talk to each other instead of queuing, and the last sentence turns reading into an argument for your project.

⚠️ **Common trap:** "Patchwriting" — keeping the original sentence and swapping synonyms ("the main
obstacle is economic rather than technical" → "the primary barrier is financial instead of
technical"). The structure and idea-order are still copied, so it is still plagiarism. Close the tab, write from your note card, then check the facts against the source.

## 3.8 AI-assisted comprehension and self-assessment

### Intuition

A chatbot's summary is a *hypothesis about the text*, not a fact about it. It is fast, fluent, and
confidently wrong exactly where the text was subtle — numbers, negations, who did what to whom. Used
well, AI is a tireless study partner: it summarises, quizzes, simplifies a hard sentence, gives a
second opinion. Used badly, it replaces the reading and you walk into the exam holding a summary of a
summary. The safe pattern never changes: **read first, summarise yourself, then compare.**

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Read and write your own 3-sentence summary before opening any tool.** That summary is your control; without it you cannot tell a good AI summary from a plausible one.

**Step 2 — Ask for a fixed shape, then diff.** "Three sentences, no opinions, keep every number." Then
compare line by line with four checks, in this order: **numbers** (did a digit change?), **negations**
(did "under-rated" become "over-rated"?), **who/what** (did actor and object swap?), **certainty** (did "may help" become "helps"?).

**Step 3 — Use it as an examiner, not an author.** Good prompts: "Give me 8 comprehension questions on
this passage, hardest last, answers hidden." "Explain this sentence in simpler English without
changing its meaning." "Which claim here has the weakest evidence, and why?" Then check the answers against the passage yourself — a wrong quiz teaches you wrong things.

**Step 4 — Never let it write your citation.** An AI can invent a reference that reads perfectly and does not exist. Every source you cite must be one you have opened yourself.

**Step 5 — Score yourself on a rubric, not a feeling.**

| Criterion | Beginner | Competent | Proficient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main idea | gets the topic, not the claim | states the claim in one sentence | states claim plus scope and limits |
| Detail accuracy | confuses similar facts | recalls key numbers | recalls numbers with their conditions |
| Inference | guesses beyond the text | separates stated from implied | justifies each inference with a line |
| Vocabulary | needs a dictionary for each unknown | uses context clues | uses morphology and context together |
| Critical stance | accepts everything | spots unsupported claims | names the assumption and the bias |
| Speed | under 150 wpm | 200–250 wpm | 250+ wpm at 70%+ comprehension |

**Example question:** A student had an AI summarise the passage below and then compared it with the original. Find every error and say which of the four checks caught it.

> *Original:* "In a 2023 trial at a Bengaluru hospital, a triage chatbot was tested on 1,200 patient
> descriptions. It matched the nurse's urgency rating in 84% of cases, but under-rated urgency in 9%
> of cases — the errors concentrated in descriptions written in Kannada. The authors concluded that
> the tool was usable only as a second opinion, and recommended further work on multilingual input."
>
> *AI summary:* "A 2023 Bengaluru hospital trial tested a triage chatbot on 1,200 patient
> descriptions. It matched nurses' urgency ratings in 84% of cases and over-rated urgency in 9% of
> cases, mostly for English descriptions. The authors concluded the tool could replace first-line
> triage and recommended more work on multilingual input."

**Solution:**

1. **Numbers first:** 2023, 1,200 and 84% all survive unchanged. *Reason: numbers are the cheapest thing to verify and the most damaging when wrong.*
2. **Negation check:** original "under-rated urgency", summary "over-rated urgency" — **error 1, direction flipped.** *Reason: a flipped direction reverses the safety implication.*
3. **Who/what check:** original errors "concentrated in descriptions written in Kannada", summary
   "mostly for English descriptions" — **error 2, a substituted specific.** *Reason: substituted details are the commonest hallucination.*
4. **Certainty check:** original "usable only as a second opinion", summary "could replace first-line
   triage" — **error 3, a hedge became a promotion.** *Reason: this reverses the paper's actual recommendation.*
5. **Judge the artefact:** two of three sentences carry errors, one of which inverts the conclusion, so
   the summary is unusable — it would have produced a wrong literature note. *Reason: judge, then decide to fix or discard.*

**Final answer:** three errors — (1) "over-rated" should be "under-rated" (negation check), (2)
"English" should be "Kannada" (who/what check), (3) "could replace first-line triage" should be
"usable only as a second opinion" (certainty check). The numbers were all correct, which is exactly why a careless reader would have trusted it.

**Why this works:** it turns "is this summary good?" into four mechanical checks that need no
expertise, and it exposes the real danger — summaries fail in the direction of sounding *more* confident than the source.

⚠️ **Common trap:** Answering exam questions from the AI summary instead of the passage. It reads
better than the original, so it feels more reliable; it is not. Summaries also flatten hedges ("may", "suggests", "in this sample"), and exams test exactly those words.

## Solved examples

**Example 1 (easy) — vocabulary in context.** In "The protocol is *lossy*: frames may be dropped when
the buffer overflows, so video plays smoothly but fine detail disappears", what does *lossy* mean and which clue told you?

Solution: (1) The colon after *lossy* announces a definition — **definition clue**. (2) The colon's
explanation is "frames may be dropped… fine detail disappears", so *lossy* = some data is deliberately
discarded. (3) The contrast with "plays smoothly" plus `so` confirms the trade: smoothness bought by
losing detail. **Final answer:** lossy = data is irreversibly discarded to gain speed or smoothness; clue = definition (colon) with cause–effect (`so`) support.

**Example 2 (medium) — reading a paper's headline number.** A paper reports "a 32% reduction in latency on 5G testbeds". What four questions do you ask before quoting that number?

Solution: (1) **Compared to what?** the authors' own earlier version is not the market leader —
*the baseline decides how impressive 32% is*. (2) **Under what conditions?** load, payload size,
distance, number of users — *a latency gain often vanishes at high load*. (3) **Measured how?**
simulation or real testbed, mean or 99th percentile — *mean latency hides tail latency, which is what
users feel*. (4) **Sample and repeats?** — *one run's 32% can be noise*. **Final answer:** baseline,
conditions, measurement method, variance; if any is missing, quote the number with its condition attached ("32% lower mean latency in the authors' simulation").

**Example 3 (exam level) — critical paragraph.** Write a 120-word critical paragraph on: "Our campus
Wi-Fi survey of 60 students shows 92% satisfaction, so the network needs no upgrade."

Model answer: "The claim rests on a satisfaction survey of 60 students, and three problems limit what
it can support. First, satisfaction is self-reported and unanchored: without a definition of
'acceptable speed', a student who only checks email and one who uploads lab videos answer the same
question differently. Second, the sample is small and its selection method is undescribed, so we
cannot tell whether dissatisfied users replied at all — non-response bias usually inflates
satisfaction figures. Third, satisfaction measures perception, not capacity: a network can satisfy
today's users and still fail a lab of 40 machines running simultaneous updates. The evidence therefore
supports 'most respondents are content with current use', not 'no upgrade is needed'. A utilisation log over one semester would test the stronger claim."

Why it works: it names the claim, gives three independent reasons (measurement, sampling, construct),
each with a mechanism, and ends with the evidence that would settle it — four marking-scheme boxes in one paragraph.

## Common traps and mistakes

- **Reading every text the same way.** One speed for a datasheet and a proof wastes hours and still misses detail. *Cost: you run out of time in exams and in project work.*
- **Confusing "not stated" with "false".** The biggest single scorer of T/F/NG marks. *Cost: 2–4 marks per passage, every attempt.*
- **Answering from general knowledge instead of the passage.** Your own knowledge of batteries is irrelevant if the passage says the opposite. *Cost: confident wrong answers.*
- **Chasing wpm without comprehension.** Speed with no comprehension figure is a vanity metric. *Cost: re-reading everything, so net time rises.*
- **Reading Method first.** You cannot judge which details matter before you know the claim. *Cost: 40 minutes on a paper that deserved five.*
- **Quoting `Typ` as a guarantee.** Datasheet typicals are not limits. *Cost: a circuit that fails in the lab and a report that is simply wrong.*
- **Patchwriting with the source open.** Synonym-swapping is still plagiarism. *Cost: an academic-integrity case, far more expensive than a bad mark.*
- **Trusting an AI summary as the text.** Flipped negations read perfectly. *Cost: wrong citations and wrong claims.*
- **Skipping the limitations section.** It is the cheapest critique available and the authors wrote it for you. *Cost: a review that reads as a summary.*

## Quick revision box

- ✅ **Skim = shape** (headings, first lines, last para); **scan = one target**; **intensive = every sentence with a pen**. Choose by task, not by text.
- ✅ **WPM = words ÷ minutes; comprehension % = correct ÷ total × 100; effective rate = WPM × comprehension fraction.** Track the third number, not the first.
- ✅ Three leaks: **sub-vocalisation, regression, no chunking** — fix with the pencil trick, a purpose per paragraph, and slash-marked phrases.
- ✅ Paper in 20 min: **abstract → headings/figures → results → method (only what you doubt) → write a 4-line card.** Most papers get discarded, correctly.
- ✅ Datasheets: **Min / Typ / Max**; `Typ` is not guaranteed and a number without test conditions is unusable.
- ✅ Unknown word: **split prefix + root + suffix**, check definition / contrast / example / cause–effect clues, then substitute the guess back into the sentence.
- ✅ Prove before you believe: critical reading separates **claim, evidence, assumption, bias** and ends in a verdict; in T/F/NG the pointer test decides — **support → TRUE, contradiction → FALSE, neither → NOT GIVEN.**
- ✅ AI loop: **read → own summary → AI summary → diff numbers, negations, who/what, certainty.** Never cite a source you have not opened.

## Practice questions

1. *(easy)* A 12-page chapter is due tomorrow and you need only the definition of "deadlock" and the four Coffman conditions. Which modes, in what order, and roughly how long?
2. *(easy)* You read 750 words in 2 minutes 30 seconds and get 7 of 10 questions right. Compute wpm, comprehension %, and effective reading rate.
3. *(medium)* In "The scheduler is *work-conserving*: it never leaves a core idle while a runnable
   task is waiting", state the meaning and the clue type, then give one reason a scheduler might deliberately *not* be work-conserving.
4. *(medium)* Write the four note-card fields (claim, evidence, limit, my comment) for: "Kulkarni
   (2021) tested a peer-review intervention in two first-year writing sections (n = 78); drafts
   improved on organisation but not on grammar; the author notes both sections were taught by the same instructor."
5. *(medium-hard)* A post says: "Since our app launched, average screen time has risen 40%, proving
   users love the new design." Write a 60-word critical evaluation naming the assumption.
6. *(exam level)* Correct this AI summary and list its errors. *Original:* "The prototype reduced
   false positives by 22% but increased false negatives, and the authors warn it is unsuitable for
   screening without human review." *AI summary:* "The prototype reduced false positives and false negatives by 22%, and the authors recommend it for unsupervised screening."

## Answers

1. **Skim → scan → intensive on one page.** Skim headings and structure (~2 min), scan for the terms
   "deadlock" and "Coffman" (~1 min), read intensively only those 2–3 paragraphs (~5 min). *Key step:* the scan is aimed at a *term*, which is why it beats reading the chapter.
2. `750 / 2.5 = 300 wpm`; `(7/10) x 100 = 70%`; `300 x 0.70 = 210 effective wpm`. *Key step:* 70% is exactly at the safe floor, so this speed is sustainable but has no margin.
3. **Work-conserving** = never idling a core while work waits. Clue: **definition (colon)**. A
   scheduler may deliberately idle a core to save energy or to keep it free for a latency-critical
   task. *Key step:* the colon supplies the meaning; the "why not" needs your own reasoning.
4. **Claim:** peer review improved draft organisation but not grammar. **Evidence:** n = 78 across two
   first-year writing sections, drafts compared before and after. **Limit:** one instructor taught
   both sections, so instructor effect is not separated; small sample. **My comment:** usable for the organisation angle only; cannot support a grammar claim.
5. Model (60 words): "The claim confuses correlation with causation: screen time may have risen
   because of a marketing push, a semester break, or a change in how time is counted. The unstated
   assumption is that users stay longer *because* they like the design, when longer sessions can
   equally signal confusion or notification spam. A comparison with a control group would test it."
6. Corrected: "The prototype reduced false positives by 22% but increased false negatives, and the
   authors warn it is unsuitable for screening without human review." Errors: (i) "false positives and
   false negatives" — the original says false *negatives* increased, so the direction flipped; (ii) the
   22% applies only to false positives; (iii) "recommend it for unsupervised screening" reverses the authors' warning. *Key step:* the negation check catches two of the three.
