## The big picture

Speaking is the skill that punishes perfectionism. In writing you can stare at a sentence for five minutes; in speech you have about a quarter of a second before silence becomes awkward. So good speakers are not people who never make mistakes — they are people whose **mistakes are cheap and rare enough not to matter**, and who keep the listener comfortable while they happen.

Here is the analogy for this module. Think of a **bus ride**. Fluency is the bus moving — a steady speed, no long stops. Accuracy is staying in your lane. Range is having more than one route. Pronunciation is the tyres: nobody praises them, but everybody notices when they are flat. Coherence is the destination board: the listener must know where you are going. Interaction is checking that passengers are still on board. A bus that stops every ten seconds, or drives beautifully to the wrong town, is a bad bus.

Where you will use this: a 2-minute answer in an interview, a group discussion with eleven other candidates, a lab viva where you must explain your code aloud, a project demo to a client, an IELTS speaking test, and the ordinary daily act of explaining an idea to a friend without confusing them.

One thing to believe from the start: **speaking improves by speaking, with feedback.** Reading about speaking improves your knowledge of speaking, which is not the same thing. That is why every sub-topic here ends with a script you should say out loud, not just read.

## Basics you must know first

- **Fluency** — the ability to keep going at a reasonable speed with few long pauses; it is about *flow*, not speed.
- **Accuracy** — correct grammar, word choice and pronunciation.
- **Register** — the formality level suited to the situation. "Give me the file" vs "Could you share the file, please?"
- **Stress** — the emphasis on one syllable in a word, or one word in a sentence.
- **Intonation** — the rise and fall of pitch across a sentence, which carries meaning and attitude.
- **Chunk** — a group of words spoken as one unit, e.g. "at the end of the day" — the natural unit of fluent speech.
- **Cue card** — the prompt card in tests like IELTS Speaking Part 2 that gives a topic and bullet points for a 1–2 minute talk.

## 2.1 Components of speaking proficiency

### Intuition

Ask a student "is your English good?" and you usually get a shrug, because "good" is not one thing. It is at least six things, and they are partly independent. You have met the person with perfect grammar who cannot hold a conversation, and the person with shaky grammar whom everyone understands effortlessly and enjoys listening to.

So the first move in improving your speaking is **diagnosis**: which of the six is actually weak for you? Fixing pronunciation when your real problem is coherence is wasted effort.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Learn the six components.**

| Component | What it means | Weak version sounds like | Strong version sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fluency** | Steady flow, few long pauses, few restarts | "I was… I mean… actually… so… the thing is…" | "I'd split it into two parts — the cost, and the timeline." |
| **Accuracy** | Correct grammar and word choice | "He don't have any idea about it." | "He doesn't have any idea about it." |
| **Range** | Variety of vocabulary and structures | "It was good. The food was good. The place was good." | "The food was excellent, the place was spotless, and the staff were unusually patient." |
| **Pronunciation** | Individual sounds, word stress, intelligibility | "I want to *wan*/*van* it" (v/w merged), flat stress | Clear vowels, correct stress in *deVELop* |
| **Coherence** | Logical order, linking, clear structure | Jumps between ideas, no signposting | "First… which brings me to… so overall…" |
| **Interaction** | Turn-taking, responding, checking | Talks over people, or goes silent | "That's a fair point — can I add one thing?" |

**Step 2 — Notice that examiners score them separately.** IELTS bands fluency and coherence together, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. A GD panel scores content, communication, and group behaviour. So a single weakness does not sink you — a *wide* weakness does. Know your profile.

**Step 3 — Diagnose yourself with a 2-minute recording.** Speak for two minutes on "a skill I want to learn". Then count: (i) long pauses over 2 seconds, (ii) grammar errors you can hear, (iii) number of different adjectives, (iv) whether you had a structure. Each count maps to one component.

**Step 4 — Fix in the right order.** For most Indian first-year students the highest-return order is: **coherence → fluency → range → pronunciation polish**. Reason: a listener forgives a wrong vowel far more easily than they forgive not knowing what you are saying. Students usually do the opposite and obsess over accent.

**Step 5 — Understand the trade-off.** Fluency and accuracy compete for attention. Under pressure, most people sacrifice one. Beginners freeze trying to be accurate; overconfident speakers become fluent and wrong. The professional balance: **be accurate on the words that carry meaning** (numbers, names, technical terms, verbs of obligation) and let small slips go.

**Example question:** A mock-interview recording of a student: *"Actually I am… I am from… my background is basically… I have done my schooling from Delhi, and… like… I am interested in coding since… since school time. So yeah, that's… that's about it."* Identify which components are weak, which are relatively fine, and give one concrete fix per weak component.

**Solution:**
- **Fluency — weak.** Evidence: three restarts ("I am… I am"), filler "like", and an abandoned ending ("that's… that's about it"). Reason: the student is composing sentence-by-sentence instead of using ready-made chunks.
- **Accuracy — mildly weak.** "I am interested in coding since school time" should be "I have been interested in coding since school" (present perfect with "since"). "I have done my schooling from Delhi" → "I did my schooling in Delhi" is more natural; "from" is a common Indian-English transfer.
- **Range — weak.** "basically", "like", "so yeah" fill space without adding meaning; no specific noun or number anywhere.
- **Pronunciation — not assessable from a transcript**, and this is worth saying in an exam: you cannot judge sounds from written text. Do not invent a judgement.
- **Coherence — weak.** The order is schooling → interest → stop. There is no point, no example, no ending.
- **Interaction — not testable here** (monologue with no interlocutor).
- Fixes:
  - Fluency: memorise five opening chunks ("I'm a second-year CSE student at…", "The reason I'm interested in…", "A quick example…"). Chunks remove the composing pause.
  - Accuracy: drill the "since/for" pair — *since* + point in time, *for* + duration.
  - Range: replace each filler with a specific — instead of "interested in coding", say "I built a attendance-tracking script in Python last semester".
  - Coherence: use point–example–point (see 2.3): "I'll answer in two parts — my background, then why coding."

**Final answer:** weak in fluency, range and coherence; mildly weak in accuracy (since/for, "schooling from"); pronunciation and interaction not assessable from a transcript.

**Why this works:** the answer separates components and refuses to guess at the ones the evidence cannot support. That last habit is what distinguishes a good analysis from a confident-sounding one.

⚠️ **Common trap:** saying "his English is bad" as a single verdict. Marks come from naming components and giving evidence per component. Second trap: judging pronunciation from a written transcript — you cannot.

## 2.2 Pronunciation, stress, rhythm and intonation

### Intuition

Pronunciation is not about sounding British. It is about **not making the listener work**. If someone has to ask "sorry, did you say *van* or *wan*?", the conversation has already lost three seconds. That is the whole target: zero repair requests.

Think of a word as a **train with one loud coach**. The loud coach is the stressed syllable; the other coaches are quieter and often shortened. If you make every coach equally loud (which syllable-timed habits encourage), the listener cannot tell which coach matters — and in English, the loud coach often carries the meaning.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Fix the five sound problems that actually cause miscommunication for Indian speakers.** These are the ones worth drilling; ignore the rest for now.

| Problem | What happens | Minimal pairs to drill | How to fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **/v/ vs /w/** | Both become a soft labiodental approximant, so *vest/west* merge | vest–west, vine–wine, veil–wail, verse–worse | /v/: top teeth touch bottom lip, and it buzzes. /w/: lips rounded, teeth nowhere near the lip |
| **/θ/ /ð/** ("th") | Becomes dental /t/ or /d/, so *thin/tin*, *then/den* merge | thin–tin, thick–tick, then–den, breathe–breed | Tongue tip lightly between the teeth, air escaping; /ð/ adds voice |
| **/s/ vs /ʃ/** ("s" vs "sh") | Confusion in spelling-driven words: *sugar*, *sure*, *measure* | sue–shoe, see–she, sort–short, mass–mash | /ʃ/: lips slightly forward, tongue a bit further back; practise *sh* in *ship* |
| **/ʒ/** as in *measure, vision* | Often replaced by /dʒ/ or /ʃ/ | vision–vishun (avoid), pleasure, garage | Rare sound; treat word by word, not by rule |
| **Aspiration of /p t k/** | Word-initial *pin/pat/cap* may lack the puff of air, sounding like /b d g/ to some listeners | pin–bin, tin–din, cap–gap | Hold a paper in front of your mouth: it should flutter on initial p/t/k in a stressed syllable |
| **Retroflex /t/ /d/** | Indian English uses retroflex (tongue curled back); English /t d/ are alveolar (tongue behind the ridge) | — | Move the tongue tip forward to the ridge behind the upper teeth; not a "fault", but it affects intelligibility abroad |

Two honest notes. (i) Retroflex /t/ and /d/ is a **feature of Indian English, not an error** — you change it for international clarity, not because it is wrong. (ii) Aspiration is the trickiest to hear in your own speech; record yourself saying "pen–Ben, ten–den, cap–gap" and listen back.

**Step 2 — Word stress: learn where the beat falls, and how it moves.**

| Word | Stress | Shift pattern |
|---|---|---|
| PHO-to-graph | 1st | base form |
| pho-TO-graph-y | 2nd | -y added, stress moves one right |
| pho-to-GRAPH-ic | 3rd | -ic pulls stress to the syllable before it |
| e-CON-o-my | 2nd | — |
| ec-o-NOM-ics | 3rd | -ics pulls stress right |
| MED-icine | 1st | — |
| me-DIC-in-al | 2nd | -al shifts it |
| a-CAD-e-my | 2nd | — |
| ac-a-DEM-ic | 3rd | -ic again |

Rules of thumb (useful, with exceptions): **-ic, -tion, -sion, -ity** pull the stress to the syllable just before them (*pho-to-GRAPH-ic*, *in-for-MA-tion*, *a-BIL-i-ty*). **-graphy, -logy, -metry** put it on the syllable before the ending (*pho-TO-graphy*, *bi-OL-ogy*, *ge-OM-etry*). Compound nouns stress the first element (*BLACKboard*, *SOFTware*); compound verbs the second (*un-DERstand*, *over-COME*).

**Step 3 — Stress also changes the word class.** This is a favourite exam item:

| Noun (stress 1st) | Verb (stress 2nd) |
|---|---|
| RE-cord | re-CORD |
| PRE-sent | pre-SENT |
| OB-ject | ob-JECT |
| IN-crease | in-CREASE |
| PER-mit | per-MIT |
| CON-tract | con-TRACT |

**Step 4 — Sentence stress: the content words get the beat.** Function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns) are weak and often reduced. So:

```text
I WANT to GO to the MAR-ket to BUY some FRUIT.
 (weak)  (strong)   (weak)          (strong)     (weak)
```

Change *which* word is stressed and you change the meaning, with no new words:

| Sentence | Stress on | Implied meaning |
|---|---|---|
| I didn't say he stole it | **I** | someone else said it |
| I didn't say he stole it | **say** | maybe I implied it |
| I didn't say he stole it | **he** | someone else stole it |
| I didn't say he stole it | **stole** | maybe he borrowed it |

This is why flat stress feels "unclear" to listeners even when every sound is correct.

**Step 5 — Intonation: two directions, five jobs.**

| Contour | Signals | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Falling** ↘ | completeness, certainty, statement, wh-question | "My name is Arjun." / "Where do you live?" |
| **Rising** ↗ | yes/no question, uncertainty, politeness, request for confirmation | "Are you coming?" / "You're from Kerala?" |
| **Fall–rise** ↘↗ | reservation, contrast, "but…" | "It's *possible*…" (implying: not likely) |
| **Rise on each item, fall on the last** | list | "We need paper ↗, pens ↗, and folders ↘." |
| **Flat / low** | boredom, annoyance, or (in some contexts) rudeness | "Fine." |

Two things that go wrong constantly in Indian classrooms: (i) wh-questions given a rising tone, which sounds uncertain — "What is your NAME ↗?" sounds like the speaker is unsure of the question; (ii) statements given a rise, which makes them sound like questions and undermines confidence. Fixing only these two makes a student sound noticeably more assured.

**Step 6 — Rhythm: squeeze the weak syllables.** English rhythm is usually described as stress-timed: stressed syllables land at roughly equal intervals and the unstressed ones get compressed. So *vegetable* is commonly three syllables in speech (VEJ-tuh-bul), *comfortable* is often KUMF-tuh-bul, and *interesting* is often IN-truh-sting. You do not have to imitate these; you do have to **not** give every syllable equal weight, or your English sounds machine-like and slow.

**Step 7 — Drill, don't read.** Ten minutes daily: minimal pairs aloud, one word-stress shift table, and one sentence said with stress on each different word.

**Example question:** (a) A student says: *"I have to present the present to my teacher."* Explain why a listener may be confused, and how stress fixes it. (b) Mark the stress in *photography*, *economic*, and *permit* (verb). (c) Give the intonation contour for: "You've finished the report ↗?" and say what it signals.

**Solution:**
- (a) The two words are spelled identically but differ in stress and word class. **PRE-sent** (noun: a gift) vs **pre-SENT** (verb: to show/give). If both are said with the same stress, the listener has to infer from grammar, which costs time and can fail. Correct production: *"I have to preSENT the PREsent to my teacher."*
- (b) *photography* = pho-TOG-ra-phy → stress on the **2nd** syllable (the "-tog-" syllable). *economic* = e-co-NOM-ic → stress on the **3rd** (the "-nom-" syllable). *permit* (verb) = per-MIT → stress on the **2nd**.
- (c) Rise ↗ on the final word, i.e. a **yes/no question contour**. It signals a genuine question expecting confirmation — and note the present perfect ("you've finished") makes it about a completed action with present relevance. If the speaker used a falling contour ↘, it would sound like a statement being asserted, or a mildly accusing remark.

**Final answer:** (a) stress distinguishes noun PRE-sent from verb pre-SENT; (b) pho-TOG-ra-phy, e-co-NOM-ic, per-MIT; (c) rising, signalling a yes/no confirmation question.

**Why this works:** it links every sound-level fact to a *communicative consequence*. In an exam, "because the listener may misunderstand which word class it is" is a much stronger reason than "because the stress is different".

⚠️ **Common trap:** marking stress on the wrong syllable because you counted letters rather than syllables — *photography* has four syllables, so stress "on the second" is "-tog-", not "-to-". Second trap: writing that a rising tone is used for wh-questions. It is not; wh-questions normally fall. Third: describing Indian English retroflex /t/ as "incorrect" — it is a variety feature.

## 2.3 Structured and extended speaking

### Intuition

An unstructured answer is like a cupboard emptied on the floor: everything is there, nothing is findable. Structure is what lets a listener *hold* your answer while you finish it. And the good news is that you do not need a new structure for each question — one pattern covers almost everything.

The pattern is **point → example → point** (PEP): say what you think, show one concrete thing, then say what it means. It is the same shape as an essay paragraph, and it works in interviews, vivas, GDs and IELTS.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Learn the three-line skeleton.**

```text
POINT:    Answer the question in one sentence. (Topic sentence)
EXAMPLE:  One specific instance, number, or story. (Evidence)
POINT:    So what? Link back, or draw the conclusion. (Landing)
```

**Step 2 — Add signposting so the listener can hear the structure.**

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Announce structure | "I'll answer in two parts…" / "There are two reasons — the first is…" |
| Move on | "That brings me to…" / "The second point is…" |
| Give an example | "For instance…" / "A quick example from last semester…" |
| Contrast | "Having said that…" / "The flip side is…" |
| Buy time (legitimately) | "That's a good question — let me think about the order." |
| Close | "So overall…" / "Which is why I'd say…" |

**Step 3 — The 1-minute talk: three chunks of about 20 seconds.** 20s point, 20s example, 20s landing. Do not attempt more than one idea.

**Step 4 — The 2-minute talk: five chunks of about 24 seconds.** Structure: *what → why it matters → one detailed example → a limitation or contrast → conclusion*. The fourth chunk is what separates a 7 from a 6 in IELTS: it shows you can see more than one side.

**Step 5 — Learn a reusable frame for the cue card.** The cue card gives bullet points; convert them into a route, not a checklist you must tick. For "Describe a skill you want to learn", the frame is: what it is → why you want it → what you have done so far → what is difficult about it → how you plan to learn it.

**Step 6 — Never memorise a full script; memorise the frame and five chunks.** A memorised script collapses the moment you forget one sentence, and examiners recognise recitation instantly. A frame survives.

**Example question:** Write the full script of a 60-second answer to the interview question: *"Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly."* Use PEP and signposting, and keep it to roughly 150–180 spoken words.

**Solution:**

> *(POINT — 1 sentence, direct answer)* "Yes — the clearest example was last semester, when I had to learn enough SQL to build a working database for our mini-project in about ten days."
>
> *(SIGNPOST + EXAMPLE — the specific story, with detail)* "I'd never written a query before. So I split it into three steps. First, two hours a day on the basics — select, where, joins — from one source only, because switching between five tutorials was my usual mistake. Second, I rebuilt the project tables myself instead of copying the schema our senior gave us, because I wanted the errors. Third, I made a list of every error message I hit, and by day six that list was my revision sheet."
>
> *(POINT — the landing, what it means)* "We submitted on time, and more importantly I can now debug a query instead of guessing. So what I actually learned was a method: shrink the scope, practise on the real problem, and keep an error log — not just SQL."

**Final answer:** roughly 170 words, three named steps, one number ("ten days", "two hours a day", "day six"), and a landing that generalises to a method rather than restating the story.

**Why this works:** the structure is *audible* — the listener hears "the clearest example was", "first, second, third", "so what I actually learned". Specific numbers make it credible without boasting. And the landing turns a small student project into evidence of a transferable habit, which is exactly what the interviewer is scoring.

⚠️ **Common trap:** answering with a *list of adjectives* ("I am a fast learner, hardworking, sincere") instead of one story. Adjectives are unfalsifiable and score nothing. Second trap: an example with no numbers or names — "I learned it quickly and it went well" tells the listener nothing. Third: running out of time mid-example because you spent 45 seconds on the point.

## 2.4 Fluency development

### Intuition

Fluency is not a talent, and it is not "thinking faster". It is mostly **retrieval speed**: how quickly your brain can pull a ready-made phrase out of storage. A fluent speaker is not composing grammar in real time — they are assembling pre-built blocks.

That is why the drills here are mechanical and slightly boring. You are not learning new English; you are moving English you already know from "recognise" to "can say instantly".

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Understand what a filler is doing.** Fillers fall into two groups:

| Kind | Examples | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Harmless / natural** | "well", "you know", "I mean", short "um" at a thought boundary | Native-like; signals thinking, not ignorance |
| **Damaging** | "like… like…", repeated "actually", "basically" in every sentence, "what I am saying is" three times | Signals panic; the listener stops tracking content |

Target: keep the first kind, delete the second. Total silence is not the goal — a 1-second pause at a boundary is better than a filler in the middle of a word.

**Step 2 — Learn three pause strategies that are better than fillers.**

1. **The stall phrase:** "That's a good question — let me take that in two parts." (buys 3 seconds, sounds confident)
2. **The honest pause:** stop, breathe, then speak. One second of silence reads as thoughtfulness; five fillers read as confusion.
3. **The rephrase-instead-of-restart:** if you get stuck mid-sentence, finish it roughly and then add "Let me put that more clearly —". Restarting from zero is what destroys fluency scores.

**Step 3 — Use the 4/3/2 technique (Paul Nation's drill).** This is the single most efficient fluency exercise known, and it is free.

```text
1. Pick a topic you can talk about (a project, your hometown, a hobby).
2. Talk for 4 minutes to a partner/listener/recorder.
3. Immediately talk on the SAME topic again for 3 minutes.
4. Immediately again for 2 minutes.
5. Compare the three recordings.
```

Why it works: the content stays the same, so the second and third attempts have nothing to invent and everything to *retrieve*. You naturally drop fillers, tighten sentences and speed up. Typical result after four weeks of daily 4/3/2: the 2-minute version contains the same content as the original 4-minute one, in half the time.

**Step 4 — Build chunk banks, not word lists.** Words are stored singly and assembled slowly; chunks come out whole. Learn families:

| Function | Chunks |
|---|---|
| Giving an opinion | "As far as I'm concerned…", "My take on this is…", "I'd argue that…" |
| Hedging | "It depends on…", "Broadly speaking…", "I'm not fully sure, but…" |
| Explaining cause | "The reason for that is…", "That's largely because…" |
| Disagreeing safely | "I see it slightly differently…", "That's true up to a point, but…" |
| Buying time | "Let me think about how to put this…", "Off the top of my head…" |

Twelve well-drilled chunks will do more for your fluency than two hundred new words.

**Step 5 — Practise thinking in English, in low-stakes moments.** Narrate ordinary actions silently: *"I'm boiling water, so I need a mug — the blue one, since the other is cracked."* The point is to remove the translation step. Translation is the main cause of the 2-second mid-sentence pause: you are waiting for your first language to hand over a word.

**Step 6 — Fluency has a ceiling you should respect.** Do not chase speed at the cost of clarity. A comfortable 130–150 words per minute with clear pauses scores better than a rushed 200 with mangled endings.

**Example question:** A student's 4/3/2 recordings show: attempt 1 = 4 min with 14 fillers and 6 restarts; attempt 3 = 2 min with 9 fillers and 2 restarts, but the same content. Diagnose what improved, what did not, and give the next drill.

**Solution:**
- **What improved:** content compression — the same material in half the time. That means retrieval got faster: the student stopped searching for what to say and started searching only for how to say it. Restarts fell from 6 to 2, so the "abandon and restart" habit is weakening.
- **What did not improve:** fillers fell only from 14 to 9, and per minute that is roughly 3.5 → 4.5, i.e. **the filler *rate* actually got slightly worse**. Reason: under time pressure, the student's filler habit became the default pause. This is a very common and easily missed pattern — always compute fillers per minute, not total.
- **Diagnosis:** fluency of *content* is improving; the *pause behaviour* is not yet trained.
- **Next drill:** a "pause substitution" drill — repeat the same 2-minute talk, but every time a filler is about to appear, replace it with a full stop and one breath. Record and count. Target: under 4 fillers in 2 minutes, then move to a new topic.

**Final answer:** content retrieval and restart-avoidance improved; the filler rate per minute worsened slightly (3.5 → 4.5/min); next drill = pause substitution on the same topic.

**Why this works:** it refuses to accept "fewer fillers overall" as progress, because the talk was shorter. Counting per minute is the honest measure, and this is exactly the kind of quantitative check an examiner's ear is doing unconsciously.

⚠️ **Common trap:** measuring fillers by total count when the duration changed. Second trap: drilling 4/3/2 on a *new* topic each time — the whole mechanism depends on repeating the same content. Third: trying to eliminate all pauses. Native speakers pause constantly; the goal is well-placed pauses, not zero.

## 2.5 Group discussions

### Intuition

A GD is not a debate and it is not a queue. It is a **group of strangers trying to build one shared answer in about fifteen minutes, while being watched.** The panel is not scoring who is right; they are scoring who is *useful to a group*.

So the mental shift that matters most: stop asking "how do I win?" and start asking "how do I move this group forward?" The person who summarises the disagreement and names the two options is usually the one who gets selected — not the loudest one.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Know the format.** Typically 8–12 candidates, a topic given 1–3 minutes to think, 10–20 minutes of discussion, then a short summary from one or two members. Topics are usually current affairs, abstract statements ("Is technology making us less social?"), or case-based ("Your company must cut costs by 20% — how?").

**Step 2 — Know the roles that exist, whether or not they are assigned.**

| Role | What they do | Selection value |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiator** | Opens with a definition or frame | High, if brief and accurate |
| **Builder** | Extends someone else's point with a new angle | Highest and safest |
| **Evidence-giver** | Adds a number, example, or case | High — most candidates do not |
| **Balancer** | Brings in the missing side | High — reads as maturity |
| **Summariser** | Names the options and the emerging agreement | High, especially late in the GD |
| **Dominator** | Speaks 40% of the time, interrupts | Negative — gets cut |
| **Silent passenger** | Says one sentence in fifteen minutes | Negative — no evidence to score |

**Step 3 — Prepare three entry lines and keep them ready.** Entering is the hardest 10 seconds; a prepared line removes the fear.

```text
Opening (if you get the first chance):
"Let me start with a quick definition, so we're all arguing about the same thing — ..."

Entering mid-discussion (polite interrupt):
"Sorry to jump in — I'd like to add one angle to what Ravi said."

Entering after a pause:
"Can I take that forward? The point about cost seems to have two parts."

Entering when two people are talking:
"I think there are two views here — could we take them one at a time?"
```

**Step 4 — Build on others, using their name.** This is the single highest-scoring behaviour. Formula: **name + their point + your addition.**

> "Building on what Priya said about rural connectivity — the part I'd add is that the bottleneck isn't cables, it's electricity."

Compare with the low-scoring version: "I think rural connectivity is bad." That is a new monologue, not a contribution.

**Step 5 — Handle a dominant speaker, without a fight.** Three escalating moves:

1. **The bridge:** "That's a strong point — let me add to it." (you take the turn by accepting theirs)
2. **The redirect:** "Before we go further, what does the group think about the cost side?" (you hand the floor to others; the dominator is now outnumbered politely)
3. **The explicit, polite appeal:** "We've heard a lot from this side — I'd really like to hear from those who haven't spoken." (this is *group-protective*, and panels notice it)

Never do: raising your voice, "let me finish!" with a hard tone, or personal remarks. All three read as a temperament problem, which is the one thing a panel will reject you for.

**Step 6 — Avoid the monologue trap.** A monologue is any turn over about 45 seconds. Even excellent content loses points after that, because you have stopped being a group member. Discipline: **two sentences, then hand over.** "…so that's my view — what do you think about the implementation cost?"

**Step 7 — Keep a mental scoreboard.** Track three things while speaking: (i) which points have been made, (ii) which side is under-represented, (iii) how long since you last spoke. Item (iii) is the one candidates forget — a good contribution at minute 2 and silence till minute 15 reads as a one-off.

**Step 8 — Prepare a closing summary shape.** "We seem to have three threads: X, Y, Z. On X there's broad agreement; on Y we split, mainly over cost. The one thing nobody has covered is implementation time." Naming an un-covered angle at the end is a strong finish.

**Example question:** In a GD on *"Should coding be taught in schools from class 6?"* the discussion is going badly: one candidate, Sameer, has spoken four times in six minutes, two others have not spoken at all, and the group keeps repeating "it will help in future jobs". Write (a) your entry line, (b) a contribution that builds on someone, and (c) a line that both handles Sameer and invites the silent members.

**Solution:**
- (a) Entry line (after Sameer's fourth turn, using the bridge): *"Building on Sameer's point about future jobs — I'd add a distinction that might help us. There's coding as a *skill* and coding as a *way of thinking*. Those are different arguments, and we're mixing them."* Reason: it enters without confrontation, and it introduces a frame the group can use — which is what "initiator" value looks like even mid-discussion.
- (b) Building contribution: *"To take Priya's point about teacher shortage one step further — if we can't staff it, the alternative isn't no coding, it's a shorter module taught by existing maths teachers, using block-based tools. That's a cost we can actually estimate."* Reason: name + their point + a new, concrete angle with a feasibility element.
- (c) Handling Sameer and inviting the silent ones: *"Sameer, you've made a strong case for the jobs angle — could we hold that and hear the other side for a minute? We haven't heard from Anjali or Farhan at all, and I'd genuinely like the counter-argument before we conclude."* Reason: it credits Sameer (so he does not feel attacked), asks for a specific behaviour change ("hold that"), and names the silent members directly — direct naming is much more effective than a general "anyone else?".

**Final answer:** enter by bridging and reframing (skill vs way of thinking), build on a named speaker with a concrete feasibility angle, and redirect with credit plus direct naming of the silent members.

**Why this works:** all three moves increase the group's output rather than your airtime. That is the actual selection criterion, and it is why a quieter, well-structured candidate often beats a dominant one.

⚠️ **Common trap:** treating the GD as a speech contest and speaking 40% of the time. Second trap: "I agree with him" with no addition — agreement without extension is scored as nothing. Third: a personal remark about a dominant speaker ("you're not letting anyone speak") which turns a content issue into a character issue.

## 2.6 Proficiency-test speaking tasks

### Intuition

Test speaking is **performance under a known format**. You are not being asked to be interesting; you are being asked to produce enough language, of sufficient variety, coherently, for a fixed number of seconds. Once you know the format, the fear drops sharply, because there is nothing to be surprised by.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Know the four task types.**

| Task | Format | What it tests | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cue-card long turn** (IELTS Part 2 style) | 1 min prep, then 1–2 min talk on a card with 4 bullets | Extended monologue: structure, range, fluency | Treating bullets as a checklist; finishing in 40 seconds; freezing on one bullet |
| **Follow-up questions** | Short questions after the long turn | Spontaneous response, opinion, justification | One-word answers; no reason given |
| **Describing a chart / graph** | Look at a visual, describe and compare | Data language, comparison, trends | Reading every number; no overall trend statement |
| **Interactive discussion** (Part 3 style) | Abstract questions, examiner pushes back | Argument, hedging, range under pressure | Repeating the same point louder |

**Step 2 — Cue card: use the 1-minute prep properly.** Do not write sentences. Write **five keywords** in a column, one per chunk of your talk, and a number beside each. Prep order:

```text
what it is          -> 1 keyword
when/where          -> 1 keyword
why it matters      -> 1 keyword
one detail/story    -> 1 keyword + 1 number
how you feel / next -> 1 keyword
```

**Step 3 — Cue card: the first sentence decides your confidence.** Use a frame, not a memorised script:

> "I'd like to talk about ___, which is something I've ___ for about ___."

That single sentence gets you 8 seconds in, and momentum does the rest.

**Step 4 — Cue card: handle a bullet you cannot answer.** Say so briefly and move: *"I don't have a strong memory of the exact date, but what I do remember is…"* Examiners do not penalise gaps in content; they penalise silence.

**Step 5 — Follow-ups: use the 2-line answer.** Line 1 = direct answer. Line 2 = reason or example. That is enough for most follow-ups, and it stops you from rambling.

> Q: "Do you think people read less than before?"
> A: "Probably less, yes — though not less *text*. Most people I know read more words per day than ever, just in messages and feeds rather than books."

**Step 6 — Describing a chart: use the four-move structure.**

```text
1. What the chart shows (one sentence, no numbers yet)
2. The overall trend (the single most important sentence)
3. Two or three specific figures as support
4. A comparison or a caveat
```

Trend language to have ready: *rose steadily, climbed sharply, plateaued, dipped slightly, fell by half, doubled, remained flat, peaked at, bottomed out at*. Approximation language: *roughly, just under, around, nearly, a little over*.

**Step 7 — Do not read the chart aloud.** "In 2019 it was 20, in 2020 it was 25, in 2021 it was 31" scores poorly. The examiner wants the *story* the numbers tell.

**Example question:** Cue card: *"Describe a piece of technology you find useful. You should say: what it is; how long you have had it; how you use it; and explain why you find it useful."* Write the full 2-minute talk (about 300 words), then write a 2-line answer to the follow-up: *"Do you think people depend too much on technology?"*

**Solution:**

> *(Opening frame)* "I'd like to talk about my noise-cancelling headphones, which I've had for about eighteen months now — they were a birthday gift from my brother, and honestly they've changed how I study."
>
> *(What and how long, briefly — do not linger)* "They're a fairly ordinary pair, nothing expensive by today's standards, but the noise cancellation is good enough to make a hostel room feel like a library."
>
> *(How I use it — the detail chunk, with a number)* "I use them in two specific ways. First, for deep work: I block out ninety minutes in the evening, headphones on, phone in another room. Second, for what I'd call 'learning by listening' — I run lecture recordings at 1.25 speed while walking to the mess, so about forty minutes a day of revision happens without me scheduling it."
>
> *(Why useful — the landing, generalising beyond the object)* "What makes them useful isn't the sound quality, it's that they remove a decision. I don't have to find a quiet place or wait for the room to empty; the quiet is portable. So the real benefit is that my study time is no longer hostage to my environment. That's a small thing, but it adds up to maybe six or seven hours a week."
>
> *(Close)* "I'd recommend them to any student in a shared room, though I'd say the cheaper models are fine — the difference is smaller than the price suggests."

Follow-up answer: *"In some ways, yes — but I'd separate dependence from convenience. Depending on a tool that makes you more effective is fine; the problem is when the tool decides what you do next. The test I use is simple: can I work for an hour without it? If the answer is no, that's dependence."*

**Final answer:** roughly 300 words; a five-chunk structure (frame → brief facts → two specific uses → generalised value → close with a recommendation); follow-up answered in two lines with a distinction and a test.

**Why this works:** it covers all four bullets without sounding like a checklist, it has one number per chunk ("ninety minutes", "1.25 speed", "forty minutes a day", "six or seven hours"), and the landing moves from the *object* to a *principle*. Examiners score range and coherence; both come from exactly this shape.

⚠️ **Common trap:** talking for only 45 seconds and stopping. In a 2-minute task, running out of content is a bigger loss than a small grammar error. Second trap: "In 2019 it was 20, in 2020 it was 25…" for charts. Third: answering follow-ups with "Yes, I think so" and nothing else.

## 2.7 Public speaking

### Intuition

Nerves are not a sign that something is wrong. They are the same physical response that makes a singer's voice carry: raised heart rate, sharpened attention, extra adrenaline. The problem is not the adrenaline; it is that we interpret it as "I am not ready".

The most useful reframe: **you are not performing for the audience, you are giving them something.** A talk is a gift with a shape — here is the thing, here is why it matters to you, here is what you can do with it. Audiences forgive a shaky voice; they do not forgive a talk with no point.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Build the opening in three moves (first 30 seconds).**

```text
Hook:     a question, a number, or a small story   (5-8 seconds)
Stake:    why it matters to THIS audience          (8-10 seconds)
Map:      "I'll cover three things: A, B, C"        (8-10 seconds)
```

Never open with "Good morning everyone, myself is…, today I am going to talk about…". It spends your most valuable 20 seconds on nothing.

**Step 2 — Use one of four reliable structures.**

| Structure | Shape | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem–Solution–Benefit** | Here is the pain → here is the fix → here is the gain | Project demos, pitches |
| **What–So What–Now What** | The fact → why it matters → what to do | Persuasive talks, seminars |
| **Past–Present–Future** | Where we were → where we are → where we are going | Reviews, annual updates |
| **Three points** | "There are three reasons…" | Almost anything short |

**Step 3 — Practise audience awareness in three questions.** (i) What do they already know? (skip it) (ii) What do they care about? (lead with that) (iii) What do they fear or resist? (address it early). A talk that answers (iii) early is trusted; one that hides it is resisted.

**Step 4 — Manage nerves with preparation, not willpower.** Four things that actually work:

1. **Rehearse the first 60 seconds and the last 30 seconds to automaticity.** Nerves peak at the start; a rehearsed opening carries you through the peak.
2. **Slow down deliberately at the start.** Adrenaline tends to speed speech up, and most speakers do not notice it; consciously slowing the first two sentences fixes most of the rush.
3. **Breathe low, once, before you begin.** One slow exhale lowers the pitch and steadies the voice.
4. **Look at one friendly face per sentence, not the whole room.** Sweeping the room with your eyes makes you feel exposed; landing on one person feels like conversation.

**Step 5 — Body language: three rules only.** (i) **Feet still, hands visible** — no pockets, no pen-clicking. (ii) **One gesture per key point**, made slightly bigger than feels natural. (iii) **Pause instead of filling** — a two-second pause after an important sentence signals confidence and gives the point room.

**Step 6 — Handle questions with a three-part reply.** Acknowledge → answer → check. *"Good question. The short answer is X, because Y. Does that cover it?"* If you do not know: *"I don't know — I'll check and get back to you by tomorrow."* This is a strong answer; inventing one is the only fatal answer.

**Step 7 — Close by returning to the opening.** If you opened with a question or a story, answer it in the last line. This makes a talk feel designed rather than assembled.

**Example question:** Write the opening 30 seconds of a 5-minute talk to first-year students titled *"Why your first project will fail — and why that's fine"*, then the closing 20 seconds. Mark the hook, stake and map.

**Solution:**

> *(HOOK — question, 6 seconds)* "How many of you have already started planning your first-year project? Keep your hand up if you're sure it will work."
>
> *(beat — pause while hands come down)*
>
> *(STAKE — why it matters to them, 10 seconds)* "I'm asking because mine didn't work. Not 'it was late' — it didn't work at all. And the useful part of that experience wasn't the failure; it was discovering that the failure was predictable, in advance, from three specific mistakes."
>
> *(MAP — 10 seconds)* "So in the next five minutes I'll show you the three mistakes, in the order they'll hit you — scope, tools, and teammates — and one habit that fixes all three."
>
> *(…body of the talk…)*
>
> *(CLOSING — 20 seconds, returns to the opening)* "Remember the hands that stayed up at the start? Almost none of them will still be up in March, and that's not because those people were wrong about themselves. It's because they hadn't yet made the three mistakes. So I'll leave you with this: your first project is not a test of whether you're good at engineering. It's a rehearsal for the version of you that will be. Fail cheaply, early, and on purpose — that's the whole skill."

**Final answer:** hook = a show-of-hands question with a pause; stake = a personal failure plus the promise of a predictable pattern; map = three named mistakes plus one fix; close = returns to the raised hands and reframes failure as rehearsal.

**Why this works:** the opening earns attention in six seconds without introducing yourself, the map gives the audience a place to put each point, and the close pays off the hook. Every one of those is a technique, not a personality trait.

⚠️ **Common trap:** "Myself is Arjun" — wrong grammar ("My name is Arjun" or "I'm Arjun") and a wasted opening. Second: reading slides aloud, which tells the audience the slides are the speaker. Third: apologising ("sorry, I'm a bit nervous, I didn't prepare much") — the audience did not notice until you mentioned it.

## 2.8 AI-assisted practice and self-assessment

### Intuition

You cannot hear your own speech the way others hear it, because while you speak you are busy producing it. A recording solves that instantly: the gap between what you *thought* you said and what you actually said is usually large, and seeing it is what makes improvement fast.

AI tools add two things to a plain recorder: a **transcript** (so you can count fillers and errors objectively) and **unlimited patience** (so you can rehearse the same answer ten times without a human getting bored). What they cannot give you is a judgement about your content or a real listener's reaction — so keep a human in the loop for anything that matters.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Record with a fixed protocol.** Same task, same duration, once a week. Otherwise you cannot compare.

```text
Weekly speaking protocol (15 minutes)
1. One 2-minute monologue on a set topic (recorded).
2. Transcribe it (AI or by hand).
3. Count: fillers, restarts, long pauses, different adjectives.
4. Re-record the SAME topic once, applying one fix.
5. Save both files with the date.
```

**Step 2 — Use the transcript for counting, not for judging.** The transcript gives you hard numbers: fillers per minute, sentences started and abandoned, repeated words. Numbers reveal patterns that memory hides. But a transcript cannot tell you about stress, intonation or eye contact — do not pretend it can.

**Step 3 — Ask AI for specific, constrained feedback.** Weak prompt: "how is my English?" Strong prompt: *"Here is a 2-minute transcript of my speech. Do three things only: (1) list every filler and its count, (2) mark grammar errors and give the corrected sentence, (3) suggest three more natural phrases for the parts I repeated. Do not rewrite my whole answer."* The constraint matters — unconstrained AI rewrites your answer into a different, more formal register you cannot reproduce live.

**Step 4 — Use shadowing and reading aloud for pronunciation.** Pick a 60-second clip of a clear speaker. Listen, then speak *simultaneously* with them, matching rhythm and stress rather than individual sounds. Then record yourself alone on the same text and compare. This is the fastest fix for flat stress.

**Step 5 — Self-rate with a rubric, monthly.** Score 1–5 on each row, and write one piece of evidence per row. Evidence is what stops the scores drifting upward for free.

| Criterion | 1–2 (needs work) | 3 (adequate) | 4–5 (strong) | My score + evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fluency** | Frequent long pauses, restarts | Some hesitation, keeps going | Smooth, natural pauses | |
| **Accuracy** | Errors block meaning | Errors noticeable but clear | Few errors, self-corrects | |
| **Range** | Same 20 words repeated | Adequate vocabulary | Precise, varied, idiomatic | |
| **Pronunciation** | Listener must ask for repeats | Understood with effort | Clear stress and intonation | |
| **Coherence** | No visible structure | Some signposting | Clear point–example–point | |
| **Interaction** | Monologue or silence | Responds when asked | Builds, checks, invites | |

**Step 6 — Pick exactly one target per fortnight.** Six criteria and six simultaneous goals equals zero progress. Choose the lowest-scoring row with the highest impact (usually coherence or fluency), drill only that, and re-rate after two weeks.

**Step 7 — Keep a human checkpoint.** Once a month, speak to a person — a senior, a teacher, a friend who will be honest — and ask one question: *"What was the one thing that made me hard to follow?"* AI will not tell you that.

**Example question:** A student's rubric reads: Fluency 3, Accuracy 3, Range 2, Pronunciation 2, Coherence 2, Interaction 3. Their transcript shows 22 uses of "good", 9 uses of "very", and every answer built as one long sentence. Choose the single fortnight target, justify the choice, and write the exact practice plan and AI prompt.

**Solution:**
- **Choose Range (2) and Coherence (2) together — but pick Coherence as the primary target.** Reason: the evidence shows one long sentence per answer, so there is no structure for the listener to hold. Range is a close second (22 "good", 9 "very"), and it is easy to improve, but fixing range without structure produces a talk full of better words with no shape.
- Why not Pronunciation first, despite the 2? Because pronunciation work needs a human or careful audio comparison and gives slower visible returns in two weeks; it is the target for the *next* fortnight, run in parallel as a 5-minute daily shadowing habit.
- **Practice plan (fortnight):**
  - Daily 10 min: answer one question using forced PEP — one sentence point, one example with a number, one landing sentence. Record. Count sentences per answer; target ≥ 3.
  - Daily 5 min: shadowing, one clip.
  - Twice weekly: 4/3/2 on the same topic.
- **AI prompt:** *"I'll send a transcript of a 90-second answer. Check only two things: (1) does it have a clear point, one example, and a concluding sentence — quote the sentences that do or don't; (2) list every repeated adjective and give me two more precise alternatives for each. Do not rewrite the answer. Then give me three follow-up questions on the same topic."*

**Final answer:** primary target = coherence (structure), secondary = range; pronunciation deferred to a parallel daily shadowing habit; plan = daily PEP answers with a sentence count, plus twice-weekly 4/3/2; prompt constrained to structure-checking and adjective alternatives only.

**Why this works:** it chooses based on *evidence* ("one long sentence"), it sequences rather than stacking goals, and it constrains the AI so the feedback is actionable by the student rather than a replacement for them.

⚠️ **Common trap:** asking AI to "improve my answer" and then memorising the polished version — it will not survive a live conversation, and it hides your real errors. Second: rating yourself 4s with no evidence. Third: changing six things at once and being unable to tell what worked.

## Formula / rule summary table

No formulas here either — this is the "which technique when" guide for speaking.

| Situation | Use this | One-line rule |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosing your speaking | Six components: fluency, accuracy, range, pronunciation, coherence, interaction | Name the weak one with evidence; do not say "my English is bad" |
| Confusing word pairs | Minimal-pair drill (v/w, th, s/sh) | One pair, ten repetitions, daily |
| Long words mis-stressed | Stress-shift families (PHOtograph → phoTOgraphy → photoGRAPHic) | -ic, -tion, -ity pull stress left by one |

| Yes/no question sounding flat | Rising intonation ↗ | Rise for confirmation, not for wh-questions |

| 2-minute talk | 5 chunks × 24 seconds | Include a limitation before the close |

## Solved examples

**Example 1 (easy) — stress changes word class.**
*Task:* Mark the stress and say the word class: (a) "The committee will *object* to the *object*." (b) "We need to *increase* the *increase*."
*Step 1:* English has noun–verb stress pairs where the noun takes first-syllable stress and the verb second.
*Step 2:* (a) verb = ob-JECT, noun = OB-ject. Sentence: "The committee will obJECT to the OBject."
*Step 3:* (b) verb = in-CREASE, noun = IN-crease. Sentence: "We need to inCREASE the INcrease."
*Step 4:* Sanity check: same pattern as REcord/reCORD, PERmit/perMIT — consistent.
**Final answer:** obJECT (v) / OBject (n); inCREASE (v) / INcrease (n).

**Example 2 (easy–medium) — rewrite a weak answer as PEP.**
*Weak:* "I am a hardworking person and I like to learn new things and I am very passionate about coding."
*Step 1:* Diagnose — three adjectives, no evidence, one long sentence, no landing. Range is thin ("very passionate").
*Step 2:* Build the point: "I'd describe myself as someone who finishes things."
*Step 3:* Add an example with a number: "Last semester four of us started a attendance-tracker project; three dropped out by week five, and I rebuilt the backend alone in nine days to keep the deadline."
*Step 4:* Land it: "So the pattern I'd point to isn't talent — it's that I'd rather ship a smaller version than abandon a bigger one."
*Step 5:* Check length: about 55 words, one story, two numbers, one claim. Better than 30 words of adjectives.
**Final answer:** point (finisher) → example (attendance tracker, nine days, three dropouts) → landing (ship small rather than abandon big).

**Example 3 (medium) — GD contribution under pressure.**
*Situation:* GD topic: "Work from home is better than working from office." You have not spoken for five minutes; two people are arguing loudly; the group has ignored cost and team culture entirely.
*Step 1:* Enter with the bridge, not a new topic: "Can I bring in the angle we've skipped?"
*Step 2:* Name the gap: "We've argued productivity both ways, but nobody has mentioned cost — for the employee, the office is a subsidised space."
*Step 3:* Add one concrete point: "A Mumbai commuter saves roughly two hours and a few thousand rupees a month; a company saves rent but pays for laptops and security."
*Step 4:* Hand over: "So it may be that WFH is better for the employee and worse for the junior who needs to learn by overhearing. Does that split hold for the rest of you?"
*Step 5:* Why this scores: it is a builder + evidence-giver + summariser move in 40 seconds, it credits the existing debate, and it ends with a question that pulls others in.
**Final answer:** bridge → name the missing angle (cost) → one concrete figure → a testable distinction (employee vs junior) → hand over.

**Example 4 (exam level) — full chart description.**
*Task:* A line graph shows average daily study hours for three groups of students (hostellers, day scholars, working students) over four years. Hostellers: 5, 5.5, 6, 6.2. Day scholars: 4, 4.2, 4, 3.8. Working students: 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5. Write a 90-second description.
*Step 1:* What it shows (no numbers): "The graph compares average daily study hours across three groups of students over four years."
*Step 2:* Overall trend in one sentence: "The clearest pattern is convergence — hostellers rose steadily while working students more than doubled, and the gap between them narrowed from three hours to under three." (Careful: 5 − 2 = 3 at the start; 6.2 − 3.5 = 2.7 at the end — so "narrowed from about three hours to under three" is accurate.)
*Step 3:* Specific figures: "Hostellers climbed from 5 to 6.2 hours. Working students went from 2 to 3.5 — the steepest relative rise, a 75% increase. Day scholars are the only group that ended lower than they started, dipping from 4 to 3.8 after a small rise in year two."
*Step 4:* Caveat: "The one figure I'd treat cautiously is the final hosteller number, because a 0.2-hour change is within normal weekly variation — the trend matters more than the exact value."
*Step 5:* Check all arithmetic: 3.5/2 = 1.75 → 75% increase ✓. 4 → 3.8 = a 0.2 fall ✓. 5 − 2 = 3 ✓, 6.2 − 3.5 = 2.7 ✓.
**Final answer:** what-it-shows → convergence as the overall trend → three supporting figures with one percentage → a caveat about precision. Note that the model never reads the twelve numbers in order.

## Common traps and mistakes

- **Saying "my English is weak" instead of naming a component.** Why it costs marks: no diagnosis means no fix, and in an exam the components *are* the answer.
- **Judging pronunciation from a written transcript.** You cannot hear sounds in text. Saying so is worth more than inventing a judgement.
- **Chasing an accent instead of intelligibility.** The target is zero "sorry, what?" requests, not a British accent.
- **Calling Indian English retroflex /t/ and /d/ "wrong".** They are variety features; you adjust for international clarity, not because they are errors.
- **Marking stress by counting letters.** Count syllables: *photography* = pho-to-graph-y (4), stress on syllable 2.
- **Rising intonation on wh-questions.** "What is your NAME ↗" sounds uncertain. Wh-questions fall.
- **Answering with adjectives instead of evidence.** "Hardworking, sincere, fast learner" is unfalsifiable; one story with one number is not.
- **A monologue in a GD (over ~45 seconds).** You stop being a group member and the panel stops scoring you.
- **"I agree with him" with no addition.** Agreement without extension scores as nothing.
- **Making a dominant speaker's behaviour personal.** It converts a content problem into a temperament problem, which panels reject.
- **Memorising a full script for a cue card.** One forgotten sentence collapses the whole thing, and recitation is obvious.
- **Reading a chart aloud number by number.** The trend sentence is where the marks are.
- **Opening a talk with "Myself is…" or an apology.** Both waste the 20 seconds that decide the audience's attention.
- **Counting fillers by total instead of per minute.** If the talk got shorter, the total can fall while the rate rises.
- **Running 4/3/2 on a new topic each time.** The drill works because the content repeats; changing topics removes the mechanism.
- **Asking AI to "improve my answer" and memorising it.** The polished version does not survive a live conversation, and it hides your real errors.
- **Rating yourself 4/5 with no evidence.** The numbers drift upward and stop measuring anything.

**Q6 (exam level).** Your self-rating is Fluency 4, Accuracy 3, Range 2, Pronunciation 3, Coherence 4, Interaction 2. Choose one target for the next fortnight, justify it with evidence, and write a five-line practice plan plus the AI prompt you would use.

## Answers

**A1.** (a) **Accuracy** — subject–verb agreement error. (b) **Range** — the same adjective three times; nothing is described precisely. (c) **Pronunciation** — the /v/–/w/ contrast is not being produced distinctly. (d) **Coherence** — no structure, no signposting, no landing (though the length itself suggests fluency is adequate, so the diagnosis must not blame fluency).

**A2.** Key step: count syllables, then apply the noun-first / verb-second pattern.
(a) **RE-cord**, noun. (b) re-**CORD**, verb. (c) **PER-mit**, noun. (d) pho-**TOG**-ra-phy, stress on the second syllable — the "-tog-" syllable, not "-to-".
Intonation: rising ↗ — a yes/no question asking for confirmation. A falling contour would make it a flat statement of fact (or sound like an accusation of lateness).

**A3.** Model answer (66 words):

> "I'd point to one project rather than describe myself. Four of us built an attendance tracker; I wrote the backend and the database schema, and when two members left in week five I also took over the testing. We shipped two days early. So what I'd claim isn't 'good at teamwork' — it's that I take the unglamorous part of a team task without being asked."

Key step: replace all three adjectives with one story, one number ("two days early") and one claim you could be challenged on.

**A4.** Model answer:
- Civil line: *"That's a strong area to bring in — and I think several of us have different pieces of it, so let's pool them rather than have one explanation."* (Credits the person, removes the put-down, avoids a fight.)
- Back-to-topic line: *"Coming back to the question — should the subsidy be universal or means-tested? That's the decision we actually have to reach."* (Replaces the personal exchange with a decision the group must make.)
Why it works: neither line argues about the insult, so the group's time is not spent on it, and both lines hand the floor back to the topic.

**A5.** Model opening:

> *(HOOK)* "Last month a teammate left our project. His code still runs in production — and nobody, including me, can explain two of the functions in it. So we're paying a small tax every week for a decision we made in a hurry."
>
> *(STAKE)* "I'm not going to argue that documentation is good. You know that. I want to argue something narrower: that undocumented code costs the *team*, not the writer — which is why it never feels urgent to the person who could fix it."
>
> *(MAP)* "Three things, in the order they'll bite you: what documentation actually is, when it pays for itself, and the one rule that gets 80% of the benefit for 10% of the effort."
>
> *(BODY STRUCTURE)* Problem–Solution–Benefit. Problem = undocumented code transfers cost to the team. Solution = comment the *why*, not the *what*, plus a five-line README. Benefit = faster onboarding and fewer repeated bugs. Close by returning to the teammate's two unexplained functions.

**A6.** Model answer:
- **Target: Interaction (2).** Evidence: in the rubric, interaction 2 means "monologue or silence" — and this is the lowest score with the highest interview cost, because a candidate who does not build on others or check understanding reads as a poor collaborator regardless of fluency. Range (2) is the second priority and can be folded into the same drill by forcing one precise word per turn.
- Justification against the alternative: Pronunciation (3) is not blocking communication; fluency (4) and coherence (4) are already adequate, so drilling them yields little.
- **Practice plan:**
  1. Daily 10 min: answer one question, then add one *building* sentence ("that connects to…") and one *checking* question ("does that answer it?").
  2. Twice weekly: 4/3/2 on the same topic, with a rule of ending each turn by handing over.
  3. Weekly: one 15-minute conversation with a senior; ask them to note every time you interrupted or went silent.
  4. Record and count: turns per conversation, and questions asked per conversation.
  5. Re-rate in 14 days with written evidence per row.
- **AI prompt:** *"Act as a conversation partner on 'my final-year project'. Ask me one question at a time and wait. After each of my answers, do not praise me — instead note whether I (a) built on my previous point, (b) asked you anything. At the end, give me the count of my turns and questions, and three phrases I could have used to hand the turn back."*
