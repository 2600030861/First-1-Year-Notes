## The big picture

Modules 1 to 5 taught four skills and then one applied skill. This module does the thing that actually gets you hired: it makes the four skills **operate together, under time pressure, in front of another person**.

**The analogy:** you have spent a year learning scales on a guitar — up, down, one string at a time. CO6 is the first time you play a song. Nothing new is being taught; the notes are the same. What is new is that they now have to arrive in the right order, at tempo, while someone watches.

**Where you will use it:** the campus placement process (aptitude test, GD, technical and HR interviews), internship applications, any certification exam for higher studies or a visa, and every working day after that — email, chat, meetings, feedback, escalation.

**The one-line summary of the module:** *listen to understand, speak to be understood, read to verify, write to be accountable — and do all four without freezing.*

**What marks actually come from.** Interviewers and GD panels are not measuring how impressive your vocabulary is. They are measuring three things: can you be understood quickly, do you know what you are talking about, and would they be willing to sit in a room with you for eight hours. Keep those three in mind for everything below.

## Basics you must know first

- **LSRW** – Listening, Speaking, Reading, Writing: the four macro-skills of language.
- **Integrated task** – one task that forces more than one skill at once (listen to a talk, then write a summary). Almost all real communication is integrated.
- **GD** – group discussion: 6–10 candidates, 8–15 minutes, one topic, no assigned leader.
- **STAR** – Situation, Task, Action, Result: the standard shape of a behavioural interview answer.
- **CEFR** – Common European Framework of Reference, the six-level scale (A1 to C2) used across Europe and increasingly worldwide to describe language ability.
- **Proficiency test** – a standardised exam (IELTS, TOEFL, PTE) that reports your English level as a score or band.
- **Portfolio** – a collected set of your own work with evidence and reflection, used to show progress rather than claim it.
- **Escalation** – formally raising a problem to someone with more authority, because you cannot resolve it yourself.

## 6.1 Integration of LSRW skills

### Intuition

Think of a canteen conversation. You **listen** to your friend, **speak** a reply, **read** the screenshot they send of the notice, then **write** a message to the group confirming the time. Four skills, ninety seconds, no separation — nobody experiences these as four subjects.

The classroom splits them because you cannot teach four things at once. Real life does not split them, and neither do exams — an interview is listening plus speaking, a GD is listening plus speaking plus reading the room, an assignment is reading plus writing.

**The loop:** input (listen, read) → processing (understand, evaluate) → output (speak, write) → feedback → back to input. Break any link and it degrades: the student who reads well but cannot summarise has a broken output link, and the student who speaks fluently but does not listen answers the wrong question — the commonest failure in interviews.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — See how each skill feeds the others.**

| This skill… | …feeds this one | How |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Speaking | You borrow vocabulary, rhythm and structure from what you hear |
| Listening | Writing | Lecture notes become the raw material of your report |
| Reading | Speaking | Reading is where your range of sentence patterns comes from |
| Reading | Writing | Structure, cohesion and register are learned by imitation |
| Speaking | Writing | Saying an idea aloud first makes it easier to write clearly |
| Writing | Speaking | Writing an outline before a talk is the cheapest way to sound organised |


**Step 2 — Use the one-task-four-skills template.** Any integrated task should have four observable outputs:

```text
1. LISTEN  -> a talk, a podcast, a lecture, a meeting      (input)
2. DISCUSS -> 5 minutes in a group of 3-4, no notes allowed (speaking + listening)
3. READ    -> a short related text, 1-2 pages               (input)
4. WRITE   -> 150-250 word summary + one critical comment   (output)
```

**Example question:** Design a 90-minute integrated LSRW task on the topic "Should colleges ban AI-generated code in assignments?" and state exactly what you would assess at each stage and how many marks each stage carries out of 20.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Set the four stages and their inputs.* Listen: a 10-minute recorded debate clip. Discuss: 15 minutes in groups of four, notes not allowed (this forces listening). Read: a 400-word opinion column taking the opposite view. Write: a 200-word summary plus a 100-word personal position.

*Step 2 — Build the time plan.* 10 min listen + 5 min individual notes · 15 min group discussion · 10 min read + 5 min notes · 30 min writing · 15 min feedback and self-assessment = **90 minutes**.

*Step 3 — Allocate marks out of 20.*

| Stage | Marks | What earns them |
|---|---|---|
| Listening notes | 4 | Five accurate key points; no invented ones |
| Discussion | 6 | Contributes at least twice; builds on someone else; brings in a quiet member; no repetition |
| Reading | 3 | Correctly identifies the column's thesis and one piece of evidence |
| Writing | 7 | 200 words in own words; summary and opinion clearly separated; no copied sentences |

*Step 4 — Add the integration twist.* Require the written piece to quote **one point made by a group member** (attributed by name) — that single instruction forces listening to be carried into writing, which is exactly the transfer this sub-topic is about.

**Why this works:** every stage has an observable output, so nothing is assessed on impression; the "no notes in discussion" rule makes listening unavoidable; and the attribution requirement welds the four skills into one artefact instead of four separate exercises.

⚠️ **Common trap:** designing an integrated task that is really four separate tasks in a row — listen, then discuss something unrelated, then read something unrelated, then write a generic essay. Integration means the *same content* flows through all four stages. If your summary could have been written without the listening, the task is not integrated.

## 6.2 Group discussions (integrated practice)

### Intuition

A GD is not a debate and not a conversation. It is a **group of strangers trying to produce one decent answer in ten minutes while being watched**. The panel is not counting who spoke most; they are watching whether the group got somewhere and whether you helped it get there.

The classic image of a GD — everyone shouting, nobody listening — is what happens when ten people each try to be the leader. The candidates who get selected are usually the ones who made the *group* better, which sometimes means speaking fourth, not first.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Know the anatomy.** Typically 6–10 candidates, 8–15 minutes, a topic given on the spot (sometimes with 2–3 minutes to think), no assigned leader, no fixed conclusion required — but a group that reaches a position scores better than one that just stops. In campus placements the panel usually has 2–3 members with a marking sheet.

**Step 2 — Know what is marked.**

| Criterion | Weight (typical) | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Content | 30% | Facts, examples, a new angle; not repetition |
| Communication | 30% | Clear, audible, structured; no fillers; listens visibly |
| Teamwork | 25% | Builds on others, brings people in, does not dominate |
| Leadership / initiative | 15% | Opens, summarises, resolves conflict, keeps time |

Weights differ by institute — ask your placement cell for the actual sheet. The important point is that **content is only about a third**: three of the four criteria are about how you behave in a group.

**Step 3 — Learn the five roles, and be ready to play more than one.**

| Role | What you say | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Initiator | "Let's take three angles: cost, ethics, and practicality." | Starting badly is worse than not starting |
| Builder | "Adding to what Rahul said about cost — the licence fee alone is ₹X." | Being invisible if you only ever add |
| Challenger | "That works in theory; what happens when the network is down?" | Sounding negative if you never build |
| Clarifier | "Can we define 'ban' first — ban in exams, or ban in submissions?" | Pedantic if overused |
| Summariser | "So we agree on two points and disagree on one — the disagreement is about enforcement." | Summarising too early kills the discussion |

**Step 4 — Have lines ready. This is the whole trick.** GDs are won with five or six prepared sentence patterns, not with brilliant ideas.

> **Opening (only if the group is silent for 3+ seconds):** "We have about ten minutes. Shall we take it in three parts — where AI code helps, where it hurts, and what a fair rule would look like?"
> **Building:** "Building on that — if the point is learning, then the test matters more than the tool."
> **Disagreeing politely:** "I see it differently, and here's why: …"
> **Bringing someone in:** "Priya, you were about to say something about the lab policy?"
> **Interrupting cleanly:** "Sorry to jump in — one quick point, then back to you."
> **Summarising near the end:** "We seem to have converged on two things: disclosure, and oral defence. The open question is enforcement."

**Step 5 — Run the full simulation.** Topic: *"Should AI-generated code be allowed in college assignments?"* Four candidates, eight minutes.

> **A (initiator, 0:05):** "We have eight minutes. Can we take three parts — where AI code helps learning, where it breaks it, and what a workable rule looks like? I'll keep time." *(Sets structure, claims a useful role without dominating.)*
> **B (builder, 0:40):** "Building on that — the useful distinction is *generation* versus *understanding*. If I can explain every line, the tool was a tutor; if I can't, it was a substitute for learning." *(Adds a frame everyone can use.)*
> **C (challenger, 1:30):** "That's clean in theory, but nobody can verify 'I understand it' from a submitted file. So the rule has to attach to something checkable — an oral defence, or a marked in-lab component." *(Attacks the idea, not the person; offers a fix.)*
> **D (silent so far, 2:15):** "…"
> **A (2:20):** "Dev, you've been quiet — what's your read on the enforcement side?" *(Brings in a quiet member. This single move is worth more than two extra content points.)*
> **D (2:25):** "Two things. One, our labs are graded on output, so students optimise for output. Two, if the rule is unclear, the honest students are penalised — they follow a rule nobody stated." *(Substantive, brief, uses "two things" structure.)*
> **B (3:40):** "I'd push back slightly on banning it outright. Industry now expects AI-assisted coding; banning it in college makes us less ready, not more." *(Disagrees with a proposal, not with a person.)*
> **C (4:20):** "Fair — so maybe the rule isn't 'ban' but 'disclose and defend'. Declare the tool, defend the code." *(Converts conflict into a formulation. Panels love this.)*
> **A (6:30):** "We're near time. Let me try to summarise: we agree AI use should be disclosed; we agree assessment should include something the student can defend orally; we disagree on whether first-year labs should restrict it. That's the open question." *(Summarises accurately, names the disagreement honestly.)*
> **B (7:10):** "Agreed, and I'd add one line — the disclosure rule should apply to faculty too, for fairness." *(One last value-add, then stops.)*

**Example question:** The GD topic is "Work from home: productivity or isolation?" Eight candidates, ten minutes. You are the fourth to speak. Write your first contribution, one disagreement, one inclusion move, and your closing summary — and say what each is doing for your marks.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — First contribution (about 25 seconds), after three people have spoken.*

> "Can I add a distinction that might organise this? We're mixing two different claims: whether people *work* well at home, and whether they *stay connected* at home. The evidence points different ways for each. Productivity data usually favours home for focused work; mentorship and onboarding clearly suffer. So the honest answer may be 'it depends on the
> task and the person's stage'." *(Content 26/30: introduces a frame, not a fact. Communication: signposted, one idea, no fillers. Teamwork: organises other people's points rather than replacing them.)*

*Step 2 — One disagreement (about 15 seconds), after someone claims "WFH always reduces collaboration".*

> "I'd push back on 'always' — our own project ran entirely online last semester and the code review actually got faster, because everything moved to written comments. What broke was the informal help, the two-minute question at someone's desk. So it's specific kinds of collaboration that suffer, not all of it." *(Challenger role, but with evidence and a
> concession. Disagrees with the word "always", not with the person.)*

*Step 3 — One inclusion move.*

> "Meera, you mentioned the internship batch earlier — was that different for them?" *(Teamwork marks: costs you nothing, and panels notice it because almost nobody does it.)*

*Step 4 — Closing summary (about 30 seconds), with about a minute left.*

> "Let me try to pull this together. We agree on two things: focused work is often better at home, and informal mentoring is worse. We disagree on whether that second point is fixable with scheduled check-ins or whether it needs physical presence. If we had to give one recommendation, it would be hybrid with fixed in-office days for the tasks that need
> proximity." *(Leadership marks: accurate, names the disagreement, produces a usable conclusion instead of trailing off.)*

**Why this works:** every contribution is *shorter than the others and better shaped* — a distinction, a concession, a question, a summary. Nothing is a monologue. The frame in step 1 makes the rest of the discussion easier for everyone, which is what "teamwork" means on a marking sheet, and the summary converts eight minutes of talk into a decision, which is what "leadership" means.

⚠️ **Common trap:** four of them. **Speaking five times with no content** — frequency is not marked. **Repeating the previous speaker in different words** — panels hear it instantly and score it zero. **Never conceding anything** — a candidate who agrees with nothing reads as uncooperative, so concede one point explicitly and then add your difference. **The fish-market trap** — if the
group is shouting, do not shout louder; pause, then use a calm "let's take one at a time" and you will stand out for the right reason.

## 6.3 Job, telephonic and video interviews

### Intuition

An interview is not an interrogation; it is a **structured conversation to reduce risk**. The company has a problem (work that needs doing) and a risk (hiring the wrong person is expensive). Every question is a way of pricing that risk. "Tell me about yourself" is not small talk — it is "give me a 90-second reason to keep listening".

Once you see it that way, the strategy becomes obvious: make the interviewer's job easy. Be clear, be specific, be brief, and give evidence instead of adjectives.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Know the structure you are walking into.**

| Stage | Length | Who | What they decide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screening (often telephonic) | 15–30 min | Recruiter / HR | Is this person real and roughly aligned? |
| Technical round 1 | 45–60 min | Engineer | Can they do the work? |
| Technical round 2 / managerial | 45–60 min | Senior engineer / manager | Can they do it with us, at our level of ambiguity? |
| HR round | 20–40 min | HR | Attitude, stability, fit, salary, joining date |
| Offer | — | HR | Terms |


**Step 2 — Answer "tell me about yourself" with a 3-part spine: now → how → next.**

- **Now (20 s):** who you are, in one sentence, tied to the role.
- **How (45 s):** two or three pieces of evidence — a project, an internship, a competition — chosen to match the job.
- **Next (15 s):** why this role, why now.

Keep it to **60–90 seconds**. This is a convention of professional interviewing, not a rule, but going past two minutes loses the room.

*Full model answer (fresher, software role):*

> "I'm a second-year Computer Science student at [college], and I've spent the last year mostly building things rather than only studying them. [*Now*]
>
> My main project is an attendance system that recognises faces — I built the detection and evaluation pipeline myself, and the part I'm proudest of is not the accuracy but the failure analysis: I found that most errors came from low light, so I added a review bucket instead of letting the system guess. Before that I interned for six weeks at a small
> startup, where I wrote the API layer for their booking flow and learned what it means to ship something someone else depends on. I also led a four-person team in our college hackathon, where we placed second — mostly because we cut our scope on day one. [*How*]
>
> I'm looking for a role where I can keep working close to the code while learning from people better than me, which is exactly why this team's work on [specific area] is interesting to me. [*Next*]"

*Why it works:* the "now" line is one sentence; the "how" gives three pieces of evidence, each with a *result* and one honest reflection; the "next" names the company specifically, which proves you read something. No adjectives about yourself ("hardworking", "passionate") — the evidence carries that.

**Step 3 — Use STAR for behavioural questions.** Behavioural questions start with "tell me about a time when…" and are asked because past behaviour predicts future behaviour.

| Letter | What goes in it | Share of the answer |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | One or two sentences of context. Who, where, what was at stake. | 15% |
| **T**ask | What *you* were responsible for. Use "I", not "we". | 10% |
| **A**ction | What you actually did, in order, with decisions and reasons. | 55% |
| **R**esult | What happened, with a number if possible, plus what you learned. | 20% |

*One complete STAR answer — "Tell me about a time you missed a deadline."*

> **S:** "Last semester our team of four had a database project due on a Friday, and on Wednesday our front-end person had to travel home for a family emergency. Two of the three screens weren't wired up."
> **T:** "I was responsible for the backend, but as the one who had set the schema, I was the only person who understood how the data was shaped, so the integration became mine."
> **A:** "I did three things. First, I cut scope with the team in fifteen minutes — we agreed to ship one screen properly instead of three badly, and I told the TA on Thursday morning rather than waiting for the deadline. Second, I wrote a thin adapter so the missing screens could be added later without touching the schema. Third, I stayed back Thursday
> night to test against the sample data the TA gave us, because our own data had been generated by the code that was broken."
> **R:** "We submitted on time with one working screen, and we lost two marks out of twenty for the missing screens — but the TA used our adapter as an example for the next batch, and I learned that raising a risk two days early is worth more than a heroic night. In the next project I built the adapter first, and we never had that problem again."

*Why it works:* the Situation is 30 seconds, not three minutes; the Task uses "I"; the Action is the bulk of it and is *specific and sequenced* ("first… second… third…"); the Result is honest — it does not claim a triumph, it claims a lesson plus a later proof. Interviewers trust the answer that admits the two marks lost far more than one that claims everything went perfectly.

**Step 4 — Communicate in technical interviews.** What is assessed is your *thinking made audible*.

1. **Restate the question** in your own words and confirm ("so the input can be empty — should I handle that?").
2. **State assumptions out loud** ("I'll assume the array fits in memory").
3. **Give the brute-force idea first**, in one sentence, then improve it ("the obvious way is O(n²); can I do better? Yes, with a hash map").
4. **Think aloud** — silence for two minutes reads as stuck, even when you are not.
5. **State complexity** when you finish, with a one-line reason ("O(n) time because one pass; O(n) space for the map").
6. **Test with a small example** on paper, including an edge case.
7. **When stuck, say where you would look** — "I'd check whether a monotonic stack applies here; can I take thirty seconds?" That is a legitimate move, not a failure. Bluffing is the failure.

**Step 5 — Telephonic etiquette.**

- Take it in a **quiet room**, seated upright, with water, your resume, the job description and a notepad in front of you. Standing up actually helps your voice sound alert.
- Confirm the caller's name and the role in the first thirty seconds: "Thanks for calling — is this about the software engineer role?"
- **Spell your name** if there is any doubt, and repeat numbers back: "that's 9-8-7-6-5…, correct?"
- If you need time: "May I take thirty seconds to think?" is fine; dead air is not.
- Ask one or two questions at the end, then confirm the **next step and timeline**: "What are the next steps, and by when should I expect to hear?"
- End with a short thank-you, not a speech.

**Step 6 — Video interviews: setup and camera presence.**

| Element | Do this | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Camera height | Lens at eye level (raise the laptop) | A low camera looks up your nose and reads as submissive; a high one reads as distant |
| Light | A window or lamp **in front of** you | Backlight turns you into a silhouette |
| Background | Plain wall, tidy, door behind you closed | Movement behind you steals attention |
| Framing | Head and shoulders, small gap above the head | Too close is aggressive; too far is a passport photo |
| Eyes | Look at the **lens** when speaking, the screen when listening | This is the single biggest video-interview mistake |
| Notes | Sticky notes at lens height, never a script | Reading is visible in the eyes |
| Dress | Full formal, including below the frame | You may have to stand up |
| Testing | Join the link 15 minutes early; keep a phone backup | Tech failure is the one excuse that never lands |

**Example question:** Answer this one, in the format above: "Why should we hire you?"

**Solution:**

*(a) "Why should we hire you?" — three beats: what they need, what you have, the fit.*

> "From the job description, the two things this role needs most are solid Java and the willingness to debug other people's code. [*what they need*]
>
> On the first: I've written Java for two years, and my last project was a 4,000-line library with tests, so I'm comfortable in a real codebase rather than only in assignments. On the second: in our hackathon, the bug that cost us four hours was in a teammate's module, and I found it by writing a failing test rather than by reading the code — which is now
> the first thing I try. [*what you have*]
>
> What I'd add is that I'm early in my career and I know it. I'm looking for a team where I can be corrected quickly, and this team's code review culture is the reason I applied. [*the fit*]"

*Why it works:* it answers the actual question (why *you*, not why the company is great), it quotes the job description so the answer is tailored rather than generic, and the last beat is honest about being junior — which reads as self-awareness, not weakness. Never say "because I need a job" and never list five adjectives.

⚠️ **Common trap:** three big ones. **Reciting a memorised "tell me about yourself"** — it sounds recited, and the follow-up question exposes it. Memorise the *spine*, improvise the words. **STAR with no R** — a story that stops at what you did, with no outcome, reads as unfinished; always end with a number or a lesson. **"We" all through the A** — the panel cannot tell what *you* did,
so use "I" for your actions and "we" only for genuinely shared ones.

## 6.4 Workplace communication

### Intuition

Workplace communication is not about being formal. It is about being **easy to act on**. Every message at work has a hidden second line: "…and here is what I need from you". The best colleagues are the ones whose messages you can read once, at speed, and know exactly what to do.

The difference from college is **volume and accountability**. In college you write for one reader who already knows the context. At work you write for a reader with 60 unread messages who does not remember your project, and your message will be forwarded to someone you have never met.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Structure email with BLUF: Bottom Line Up Front.**

```text
Subject:   [Action needed by Thu 21 Nov] Lab 3 booking for coding club
Line 1:    The ask, in one sentence.
Line 2-4:  Why, and the minimum context needed.
Line 5:    What I need from you, by when.
Sign-off:  Name, role, phone
```

**Subject lines carry the message.** "Regarding the thing we discussed" is a subject that guarantees a delayed reply. "Approval needed: Lab 3, Saturdays 4–5, from Dec" is a subject that can be answered from the notification bar.

*Before/after rewrite:*

> **Before:** "Respected Sir, I hope this mail finds you in good health. I am writing this mail to bring to your kind notice that we had a discussion last week regarding the coding club and you had told us to mail you the details, so I am mailing the details. We are planning to start a coding club in our college for the benefit of all the students so that
> they can improve their coding skills which will help them in placements. Kindly do the needful and please reply."
>
> **After:** "Subject: Approval needed by Fri 22 Nov — Lab 3, Saturdays 4–5, coding club
>
> Sir, I need your approval on three points so we can start the coding club on 1 December.
>
> 1. Room: Lab 3, Saturdays 4–5 p.m. (currently unused at that time).
> 2. Mentors: two faculty, names attached.
> 3. Notice: one line on the department board.
>
> The club runs one hour a week: basics, contests, interview sets. Cost to the department: zero. A one-page note and the signature sheet are attached.
>
> Could you confirm by Friday 22 November? If any of the three is a problem, tell me which and I'll rework that part only.
>
> Rahul Menon, 3rd year CSE, 98xxxxxxx"

*Why the after works:* the subject is the ask plus a date; the first line states the decision needed; the three points are numbered so they can be answered "yes to 1 and 3, no to 2"; the deadline is explicit; and the close makes it easy to say no to one item instead of the whole thing. The before-version has no subject line, no ask, no deadline, and "kindly do the needful" — a phrase
that transfers the thinking back to the reader.

**Step 2 — Follow email norms.** One topic per email. Reply within one working day — a common convention, not a law; if you need longer, send a one-line holding reply. Use "reply all" only when everyone needs it. Name attachments properly ("CodingClub_Proposal_v2.pdf", never "doc1.pdf"). Quote only the relevant part of a long thread. Never send an email when you are angry — write it,
wait an hour, edit. Put the sensitive part in the *first* paragraph, because people stop reading.

**Step 3 — Chat and IM norms.** Chat is for quick, low-stakes, time-sensitive things; email is for decisions and records. **No "hi" then waiting** — send "Hi, quick question about the deploy script: does it run on staging?" in one message. One thread per topic. Respect status ("in a meeting" means expect a delay). Do not send bad news by IM — a missed deadline belongs in a call or an
email where tone is not lost. And remember chat is a record: write as if it will be screenshotted, because it can be.

**Step 4 — Behave well in meetings.** Arrive on time; read the agenda beforehand. Speak once early if you have something — early contributions are heard, late ones are rushed. One person speaks at a time. Take your own action items down in the meeting, then send them in a two-line recap ("Confirming: I'll send the dataset by Wednesday; Priya will check the licence"). Do not hijack: if a
topic is not yours, ask a question rather than starting a parallel discussion. If you have nothing to add, "I'll take that offline" is a complete contribution.

**Step 5 — Give feedback with SBI, receive it without defending.**

| Model | Parts | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | When and where | "In yesterday's review…" |
| **B**ehaviour | What was observable, not your interpretation | "…the status update ran 12 minutes past the slot and two people's questions were cut." |
| **I**mpact | The effect, on the work or the team | "…so the risk item never got discussed, and we're now deciding it by email." |

Notice what is *absent*: "you are always disorganised" (a judgement about a person), "you don't care" (a guess about motives). Feedback that contains a character judgement gets defended against; feedback that contains a behaviour gets acted on.

*Receiving feedback:* listen without interrupting, ask for a concrete example if none was given ("can you point to the instance?"), thank the person, and say what you will do differently — then actually do it. Do not explain why you did it; the explanation reads as a defence even when it is true.

**Step 6 — Communicate across teams.** Use the other team's vocabulary, not yours ("the student record table" not "the `students` entity"). Write a one-paragraph recap after any cross-team call, because shared understanding decays within a day. Always name a single owner for each action, never "the team will". And when two teams disagree, put the disagreement in writing with both
positions — most cross-team conflicts are actually two different assumptions, and writing them down exposes that.

**Step 7 — Escalate properly.** Escalate when you are blocked and cannot unblock yourself, when a deadline is genuinely at risk, when a decision is above your authority, or when something is ethically wrong. Do not escalate to punish someone or to avoid a difficult conversation.

The four-part escalation, in this order: **facts → impact → options → ask.**

> "The staging server has been down since 10 a.m. (facts). That blocks testing for the release on Friday (impact). Options: roll back last night's deploy, or use the backup instance which is a version behind (options). I'd like approval to roll back — it's the faster path, and it costs us one evening of re-testing (ask)."

Skip-level escalations (going above your manager) are legitimate but carry a cost: tell your manager first, always, even if you disagree with them.

**Example question:** Rewrite this message as a professional escalation, and say what you changed: *"Hi, I have told you twice already that the API key is not working and nobody is replying, this is very unprofessional and because of this our whole module is delayed. Please look into it immediately."*

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Diagnose.* The facts are buried under emotion; there is no impact quantified, no options, no specific ask, and "this is very unprofessional" attacks a person, which guarantees a defensive reply. There is also no deadline, so it can be read tomorrow.

*Step 2 — Rewrite using facts → impact → options → ask.*

> "Subject: Blocked — staging API key failing since Monday (module due Fri 22)
>
> Hi Sana,
>
> The staging API key returns 401 on every call from our side; I've attached the request ID and the two timestamps. I raised it on the team channel on Monday and Wednesday, so I'm escalating to you as the owner of that service.
>
> Impact: our payments module can't be integration-tested, and it's due Friday. If the key isn't sorted by Thursday noon, we'll release without the integration test and flag it as a known gap — which I'd rather avoid.
>
> Two options on our side: (1) you issue a new key today and we retest tomorrow, or (2) we use the sandbox key with reduced scope. Option 1 is cleaner; option 2 unblocks us immediately.
>
> Can you confirm which by Thursday 10 a.m.? Happy to jump on a five-minute call if it's faster.
>
> Rahul Menon, CSE, 98xxxxxxx"

*Step 3 — What changed.* Facts with evidence (401, timestamps, request ID) instead of "not working"; a timeline of prior attempts (so it is a record, not a complaint); impact with a consequence and a date; two options, with a recommendation; a specific ask with a time; and the accusation removed — the recipient is now a person who can solve a problem rather than a person being blamed.

**Why this works:** escalation is not a complaint, it is a **decision request**. Every part of the rewrite gives the reader something to act on, and the "I'd rather avoid" line makes the cost of inaction visible without threatening anyone.

⚠️ **Common trap:** "kindly do the needful" and "please look into it" — both transfer the thinking back to the reader and, in a busy inbox, they are the messages that get postponed. Also: escalating before you have tried the direct channel (which destroys trust), and putting a complaint in writing with no facts (which is a record of your frustration, not of the problem).

## 6.5 English-proficiency certification and CEFR levels

### Intuition

CEFR is a **ruler**, not a prize. It answers one question: what can you actually *do* in English right now? The six levels are not percentages and not marks; they are descriptions of capability, and the point of them is that a university in Germany and an employer in Bengaluru can read the same label and mean the same thing.

Proficiency tests are **different rulers measuring the same height**. IELTS, TOEFL and PTE each produce a score on their own scale, and all three publish an approximate conversion to CEFR. That is why a PTE 68 and an IELTS 6.5 can both mean "B2" — the number looks different because the ruler is different.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Learn the six CEFR levels as "can do" statements.**

| Level | Name | Can do, in practice | Typical marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Breakthrough | Understands and uses very basic phrases; introduces themselves; asks and answers simple questions about familiar topics if the other person speaks slowly | Can handle shop, directions, form-filling |
| **A2** | Waystage | Handles short, routine exchanges; describes their background in simple terms; understands short simple texts | Can manage a tourist trip; struggles with a work meeting |
| **B1** | Threshold | Copes with most everyday situations; describes experiences and gives short reasons; understands the main point of clear standard input | Can follow a familiar lecture with effort; writes simple connected text |
| **B2** | Vantage | Interacts with fluency and spontaneity in most situations; argues a viewpoint with advantages and disadvantages; understands technical discussion in their own field | Can work in English with support; can study in English |
| **C1** | Effective operational proficiency | Uses language flexibly and effectively for social, academic and professional purposes; produces well-structured, detailed text; understands implicit meaning | Can work independently in English; can present and negotiate |
| **C2** | Mastery | Understands with ease virtually everything heard or read; expresses themselves precisely, differentiating finer shades of meaning | Near-native command; handles high-stakes writing |

Two honest notes. First, these are paraphrases of the official descriptors, not the official wording — the full "can do" tables are long, and exam boards publish their own. Second, **levels are uneven across skills**: a very common profile is B2 reading and writing with B1 speaking, which is why some universities ask for a minimum *per section* and not only an overall score.

**Step 2 — Map the three big tests to CEFR.** These are the test owners' own indicative mappings; treat them as approximate bands, not exact conversions.

| CEFR | IELTS (overall band) | TOEFL iBT (total, out of 120) | PTE Academic (out of 90) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C2 | 8.5–9.0 | 114–120 | 85–90 |
| C1 | 7.0–8.0 | 95–113 | 76–84 |
| B2 | 5.5–6.5 | 72–94 | 59–75 |
| B1 | 4.0–5.0 | 42–71 | 43–58 |
| A2 | 3.0–3.5 | below 42 | 30–42 |

In brief: IELTS Academic is paper or computer, about 2 h 45 min, band 0–9 in half steps, with a face-to-face Speaking test. TOEFL iBT is computer-based in one sitting of about 2 hours, scored 0–120 (0–30 per section), with Speaking recorded. PTE Academic is fully computer-based, about 2 hours, scored 10–90, with several integrated tasks and machine scoring. Exact formats, section counts
and timings are revised from time to time — check the official test website before you book, and check the specific requirement of the university or employer, because institutions set their own cut-offs (a common Master's requirement is "IELTS 6.5 overall, no band below 6.0"). Scores are typically valid for two years, and some institutions ask for a test taken within a shorter window.

**Step 3 — Translate a band into "what it means in practice".**

| IELTS band | What it usually means for you |
|---|---|
| 5.5 | You can survive daily life and simple coursework, but lectures and group work will be hard work |
| 6.0–6.5 | You can study in English, with effort; expect to re-read a lot and to be quiet in fast seminars |
| 7.0 | You can follow most lectures, argue a point, and write a coherent essay; occasional idiom gaps |
| 8.0+ | Comfortable across academic and professional registers; errors are rare and non-disruptive |

**Example question:** A student has three score reports: IELTS overall 6.5 (with Writing 5.5), TOEFL iBT 90, and PTE Academic 68. (a) Place each on the CEFR scale. (b) A university asks for "CEFR B2 overall, with no section below B2". Does the student qualify on each report? (c) What would you advise?

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Place each score using the mapping table.* IELTS 6.5 → **B2** (5.5–6.5). TOEFL 90 → **B2** (72–94). PTE 68 → **B2** (59–75). All three say the same thing in three different units, which is the point of CEFR.

*Step 2 — Check the per-section condition.* On the IELTS-to-CEFR mapping, 5.5 is the first band of B2, not B1 — so the CEFR label alone hides the problem. Universities usually write the rule against section *bands* ("no band below 6.0"), so the practical check is whether any section falls below that floor. Here Writing 5.5 is below a typical 6.0 floor, so on that wording the student
**fails the writing condition** even though the overall band is fine. TOEFL 90 and PTE 68 are overall figures; the same per-section check applies to their section scores, which this question does not give.

*Step 3 — Advise.* Read the requirement literally and match it section by section, not by overall band. If Writing is the constraint, retake with a writing-focused plan (or choose the test whose writing task plays to your strength) rather than retaking blindly. And confirm with the admissions office in writing, because institutions convert scores using their own tables.

**Final answer:** (a) all three are B2 overall. (b) overall requirements are met on all three; the "no section below" condition fails on IELTS Writing 5.5 against a typical 6.0 floor, and cannot be verified for the other two without section scores. (c) advise a targeted retake or a formal written confirmation from admissions.

⚠️ **Common trap:** treating a band as a percentage (IELTS 7 is not "70%"), and treating the CEFR mapping as official for your institution. Mappings are indicative; universities publish their own conversion and their own cut-offs, and a claim like "PTE 68 always equals IELTS 6.5" is a convention, not a guarantee.

## 6.6 Practice tests and strategies

### Intuition

A proficiency test does not measure your English; it measures **your English under that test's specific rules, in that time limit**. This is why a fluent speaker can score 6.0 while a less fluent classmate scores 7.0: the second one learned the format. Format knowledge is worth roughly half a band, and it is the cheapest half-band available.

The second principle: **practice tests are diagnostic tools, not workouts.** Doing five tests without reviewing your mistakes teaches your mistakes to you. One test reviewed thoroughly is worth three tests done.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Take one full test cold and mark it honestly.** You need a baseline before a plan. Log every wrong answer with a *reason code*: spelling, misread question, distractor, ran out of time, unknown word, answered from opinion instead of text.

**Step 2 — Apply section-wise strategy.**

| Section | Core strategy | The one habit that moves the score |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Read the questions before the audio; predict what kind of answer is coming (a number? a name? a reason?) | Write the answer as you hear it — do not wait for the end |
| Reading | Skim the passage in 90 seconds for the gist, then answer question by question, scanning for the location | Never read the whole passage at full speed first; you do not have the time |
| Writing | Spend 3–5 minutes planning before writing; one clear position, one idea per paragraph | Answer the exact question — off-topic essays score low however good the English |
| Speaking | Part 2 (IELTS): use the 1 minute to write 4 keywords, not sentences; then speak for the full 2 minutes | Keep talking; silence is scored as nothing |

**Step 3 — Manage time explicitly.** For IELTS Academic Reading: 40 questions in 60 minutes, so about **20 minutes per passage** including transfer — that is 90 seconds per question. If a question resists after 90 seconds, mark it, move on, come back. For IELTS Writing: Task 1 in 20 minutes, Task 2 in 40, because Task 2 carries about twice the weight of Task
1. In TOEFL, the sections are separately timed and you cannot borrow minutes between them, so a slow start is unrecoverable. In PTE, tasks are individually timed, so the discipline is per task, not per section.

**Step 4 — Know the common score-killers.** These cost more marks across candidates than lack of vocabulary does.

- **Spelling in Listening** — "accommodation" misspelled is a wrong answer. Spelling counts.
- **Word count** — under the minimum in Writing costs marks directly (IELTS Task 1 needs 150 words, Task 2 needs 250).
- **True / False / Not Given confusion** — FALSE means the passage contradicts the statement; NOT GIVEN means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it. If you had to reason it out from outside knowledge, it is NOT GIVEN.
- **Memorised Speaking answers** — examiners are trained to spot them and they score lower than imperfect spontaneous answers.
- **Leaving blanks** — there is no negative marking in IELTS and TOEFL, so a guess costs nothing and can gain a mark. Always answer.
- **Ignoring the question type** — "discuss both views and give your opinion" needs both views *and* your opinion; give only one and you cap your own band.
- **Practising full tests without reviewing** — the most expensive habit of all.

**Example question:** A candidate has taken IELTS twice and scored 6.0 both times, with Listening 6.5, Reading 6.5, Writing 5.5, Speaking 5.5. They want 7.0 overall. Build a realistic six-week plan and say what is *not* worth doing.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Do the arithmetic first.* IELTS overall band is the average of the four sections, rounded to the nearest half band. Current: (6.5 + 6.5 + 5.5 + 5.5) / 4 = 24 / 4 = **6.0**. To reach 7.0 the average must be at least 6.75 (which rounds up to 7.0). So the four sections must total **at least 27**. That means gaining **3 band points** across the sections.

*Step 2 — Choose the cheapest three points.* Writing and Speaking are 5.5 each and are the weakest; Listening and Reading are already 6.5. A realistic target: Writing 5.5 → 6.5 (+1), Speaking 5.5 → 6.5 (+1), Listening 6.5 → 7.0 (+0.5), Reading 6.5 → 7.0 (+0.5). Total 27 → average 6.75 → **overall 7.0**. This is the plan; it does not require a 7.0 in any single section.

*Step 3 — Six-week plan.*

| Week | Focus | Weekly hours |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Full diagnostic under exam conditions; build the error log; learn the Writing Task 2 question types | 8 |
| 2 | Writing: one Task 2 per day, 40 min timed, then rewrite after feedback; Speaking: record Part 2 answers, 2 min each, listen back | 10 |
| 3 | Writing Task 1 (data description language) + Speaking Part 3 (opinion and speculation); one Listening section test | 10 |
| 4 | Full test under exam conditions; review every error by type; fix the top two error types | 10 |
| 5 | Two timed Reading section tests + two Writing tasks; one Speaking mock with a teacher or a fluent friend | 8 |
| 6 | Light practice only; revise the error log; two Speaking mocks; sleep and timing routine | 5 |

*Step 4 — What is NOT worth doing.* Not more full tests (they diagnose, they do not teach). Not memorising essay templates — examiners discount them and they collapse when the question does not fit. Not learning advanced vocabulary lists; at 5.5 the problem is almost always **task response and coherence**, not word choice. Not ignoring Speaking because it feels uncomfortable — it is one
of the two sections that must move.

**Why this works:** it starts from the arithmetic of the band calculation, so the target is derived rather than wished for; it attacks the two weakest sections where a full band is available, instead of grinding the two that are already close to target; and it separates *diagnostic* activity (tests) from *improvement* activity (targeted practice with feedback).

⚠️ **Common trap:** grinding full practice tests because they feel productive. They raise your *familiarity* and nothing else after the second one. Also: assuming overall band 7.0 requires 7.0 in every section — it does not, it requires an average of 6.75, which is why section-by-section arithmetic is the first thing to do.

## 6.7 LSRW portfolio development

### Intuition

A portfolio is not a scrapbook; it is an **argument about yourself, made with evidence**. Anyone can write "I have improved my speaking". A portfolio says: here is a recording from August, here is one from November, here is my feedback sheet, here is what I changed, here is the difference.

The principle is the same as a Git history. A claim is a commit message; a portfolio is the diff.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Collect the right evidence, four or five items per skill.**

| Skill | What to collect | How much |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Listening logs: source, date, 5 key points, 1 new expression, 1 thing you disagreed with | 6–8 logs |
| Speaking | Recordings of 2–3 minutes (presentation extract, GD contribution, interview answer) | 4–5 recordings, spread over the semester |
| Reading | Summaries of 2–3 academic or news texts, 150–200 words each, plus one critical comment | 4 summaries |
| Writing | One email, one short report or proposal, one argumentative paragraph, one corrected draft showing revision | 4 pieces |

Include the **feedback** as well as the work: a rubric score, a teacher's comment, a peer comment. Evidence of improvement needs a before and an after.

**Step 2 — Write each entry in a fixed shape, so the portfolio reads consistently:** title, task, what I did (3–4 specific lines), evidence (file, date, who gave feedback), what I learned (including what still fails), and one next action.

**Step 3 — Build the document in 6–8 pages:** cover page, contents, a 150-word introduction on where you started, the evidence table, four entries in the shape above, a 300-word reflection, and 150 words of goals for next semester.

**Step 4 — Make the reflection do the work.** The reflection is where marks are won, because it is the only part that cannot be copied. Use before/after pairs with specifics: "In August I used about ten fillers a minute in recordings; in November it was three, measured by listening to both at 1.25x. What changed was that I started pausing instead."

**Step 5 — Keep it honest.** Do not include work you did not do, and do not describe a failure as a success. A portfolio that says "this recording is still weak on eye contact; I am working on it" is stronger than one that claims perfection, because it proves you can assess your own work — which is the actual skill being assessed.

**Example question:** Write the evidence table for a semester portfolio plus one full entry (Speaking), for a student who wants to show improvement in presentations.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — The evidence table.*

| Skill | Item | Date | Evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Listening | Listening log — technical talk on databases | 5 Aug | log_01.md | Can extract five key points from a fast talk |
| Listening | Listening log — GD recording of my own group | 20 Sep | log_04.md | Can hear my own interruptions; noticed I cut in twice |
| Speaking | Presentation extract, project review | 28 Aug | speak_01.mp3 | 11 fillers/min, read from slides |
| Speaking | Presentation extract, second review | 12 Nov | speak_04.mp3 | 3 fillers/min, three eye-contact anchors used |
| Reading | Summary — news article on AI in agriculture | 15 Sep | read_02.md | Can separate claim from evidence |
| Reading | Summary — paper abstract with critique | 30 Oct | read_04.md | Can question a method, not just summarise it |
| Writing | Email requesting lab access | 2 Sep | write_01.md | Clear ask and deadline, but too long |
| Writing | Revised email after feedback | 6 Sep | write_01_rev.md | Cut from 180 to 90 words; BLUF added |

*Step 2 — One full entry.*

> **Speaking — project review presentation, 12 November**
> *Task:* Present a five-minute project update to two faculty and answer questions.
> *What I did:* Scripted the first and last 60 seconds word for word, kept the middle on four keyword cards, and rehearsed three times with a timer, recording the second run. In Q&A I used the pause-repeat-answer-check loop for both questions.
> *Evidence:* speak_04.mp3 (recording), rubric sheet scored 74/100 by the reviewer, my own self-assessment notes from 13 Nov.
> *What I learned:* Fillers dropped from 11 per minute in August to 3, mostly because I replaced them with a half-second pause. I also learned that my biggest remaining weakness is the middle section: without a card I drift, and the second body point ran 40 seconds long. The reviewer's comment "you were much clearer on the opening than on the third point"
> matched my own recording.
> *Next action:* Rehearse the middle with the same discipline as the opening — one card per claim, and a visible timer on the desk for the next review.

**Why this works:** the entry contains a *measurable* claim (11 → 3 fillers per minute), names its evidence so anyone can check it, admits the remaining weakness with a specific observation, and ends with one action rather than a wish. That is the difference between a portfolio and a folder.

⚠️ **Common trap:** a portfolio that is 40 pages of certificates with no reflection. Certificates prove attendance; the reflection proves learning. Also: collecting everything and selecting nothing — a portfolio is curated, so four good entries beat twenty unlabelled files.

## 6.8 Lifelong learning and integrated mock assessment

### Intuition

Your English will not stay where it is. Two forces act on it: **use** (a job that makes you write daily will pull you up) and **decay** (a year of only reading textbooks will pull you down). Lifelong learning here does not mean courses; it means a **maintenance routine** small enough that you will actually keep doing it.

The second idea is the mock assessment: you cannot improve what you have never measured under realistic conditions. A mock is a rehearsal of the *whole* loop — listen, discuss, read, write, interview — not of one skill in isolation.

### The idea, step by step

**Step 1 — Build a maintenance routine you will keep.** Thirty minutes three times a week beats a two-hour Sunday session that happens twice: 10 minutes listening (a talk with a transcript), 10 minutes speaking aloud (record 2 minutes on what you heard), 10 minutes writing (a 100-word summary). Add one hour a week of reading something longer than an article.

**Step 2 — Practise deliberately, not just more.** Pick one weakness, work on it with feedback, at the edge of your ability. "Watch more English videos" is exposure; "record a 2-minute answer, listen for fillers, re-record" is practice, because it has a target and a check.

**Step 3 — Build feedback loops you can run yourself.** A recording plus a rubric is one. So is an error log: the sentence, the correction, the *type* of error — three columns, and after a month the pattern is undeniable. AI tools help here (transcribing, counting fillers, tightening a sentence) as long as you treat the output as a draft opinion, not the truth.

**Step 4 — Run an integrated mock assessment.** Ninety minutes, four stages, scored.

| Stage | Time | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listening | 15 min | 6-minute talk, then write 5 key points + 1 question you would ask | 15 |
| Group discussion | 20 min | 4–6 people, one topic, marked on the 6.2 table | 25 |
| Reading + writing | 30 min | 1-page text, then a 200-word summary + 100-word opinion | 25 |
| Interview | 20 min | 8 min technical/HR questions + 5 min Q&A + 5 min feedback | 35 |
| Self-assessment | 5 min | Fill the self-rating sheet | — |

**Step 5 — Fill a self-rating sheet, and date it.** Rate 1–5, where 1 = "I avoid this", 3 = "I can do it with preparation", 5 = "I can do it cold, under time".

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I can give a 2-minute impromptu answer with a clear structure | | | | | |
| I can answer "tell me about yourself" in 90 seconds, tailored to the role | | | | | |
| I can give a STAR answer with a quantified result | | | | | |
| I can contribute three substantive points in a 10-minute GD | | | | | |
| I can bring a quiet member into a discussion without awkwardness | | | | | |
| I can write a professional email with the ask in the first line | | | | | |
| I can follow a fast talk in an unfamiliar accent and take usable notes | | | | | |
| I can handle a question I don't know without bluffing | | | | | |
| I can receive critical feedback without defending myself | | | | | |

The two lowest-scoring rows are your next month's plan. Re-rate after four weeks; the numbers should move, and if they do not, the practice was not deliberate.

**Example question:** A second-year student rates herself 2 on "STAR answer with a quantified result", 3 on "GD with three substantive points", and 4 or 5 on everything else. Build her 90-minute mock assessment plan for the next four weeks and say how she will know it worked.

**Solution:**

*Step 1 — Turn the two weak rows into two practice targets.* Target 1: produce six STAR answers with a real number in the R. Target 2: three substantive contributions per GD without repeating anyone.

*Step 2 — Weekly 90-minute mock, same shape every week so progress is comparable.*

| Week | Mock focus | Extra drill during the week |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Full mock as a baseline; record the interview stage | Write 3 STAR stories from her own projects (deadline, conflict, failure) |
| 2 | Interview stage only, 20 min, 8 questions, scored on the rubric | Re-record the weakest STAR answer twice; add a number to each R |
| 3 | GD stage only, 20 min with 4 peers, marked on the 6.2 table | Prepare five "building" lines and use them; count repetitions in the recording |
| 4 | Full mock again, same conditions, same rubric | Compare week 1 and week 4 recordings side by side |

*Step 3 — Define "it worked" before starting.* Success is not "felt better". It is: (a) all six STAR answers contain a specific number and a lesson in the R; (b) in the week-4 GD recording, at least three of her contributions add something no one had said; (c) her self-rating on those two rows moves from 2 to 3, and a peer scores her at least 3 on the same items using the rubric.

*Step 4 — Build the feedback loop.* After each mock: one recording, one rubric, one written "one thing to change". No more than one change per week, or the comparison across weeks means nothing.

**Why this works:** the plan is derived from the two lowest self-ratings rather than from a generic syllabus; every week has a measurable output that can be compared across weeks; and success is defined in observable terms before the practice starts, which is what turns "practice" into "deliberate practice".

⚠️ **Common trap:** rating yourself 4 or 5 on everything, which makes the sheet useless — the sheet is only valuable where it is uncomfortable. And the related trap: practising only what you are already good at, because it feels productive. The two lowest rows are the whole point.

## Solved examples

### Example 1 (medium) — the 90-second interview answer, rebuilt

**Task:** A student's answer to "tell me about yourself" is: *"Sir, I am Rahul from Chennai. I completed my 12th in 2023 with 89%. I am interested in coding and I have done many projects. I am a hardworking person and a quick learner. I like to play cricket. Thank you."* Rebuild it.

**Solution:** *Step 1 — Diagnose.* No structure (it is a list). No evidence ("many projects" — which ones?). Three adjectives doing the work that evidence should do ("hardworking", "quick learner"). Personal hobbies last and unexplained. No connection to the role. Roughly 25 seconds long — under-length, which reads as unprepared.

*Step 2 — Rebuild with now → how → next, in about 75 seconds (≈170 words).*

> "I'm a second-year CSE student in Chennai, and for the last year I've mostly been building things rather than only doing coursework. [*now, 15 s*]
>
> The one I'd point to is a face-recognition attendance system — I wrote the detection and evaluation code myself, and the part I actually learned from was the failure analysis: most errors were low-light frames, so instead of pushing accuracy I added a review bucket so the system flags uncertainty instead of guessing. I also interned for six weeks at a
> startup, where I built the API layer for a booking flow and learned what it means when someone else's code depends on yours. And I led a four-person team in a college hackathon where we came second — mainly because we cut our scope on day one instead of day three. [*how, 45 s*]
>
> I'm looking for a team where I can be corrected quickly while working close to the code, which is why this role interested me. [*next, 15 s*]"

*Step 3 — Check against the diagnosis.* Structure: three labelled beats. Evidence: three specific items, each with a decision and a result. Adjectives: removed entirely — the hackathon placement and the failure-analysis decision *show* the qualities instead of claiming them. Role connection: the last line names what he wants and ties it to the role.

**Final answer:** 15 s now + 45 s how + 15 s next, with three evidence items and no self-praise adjectives. The cricket line is not banned — it belongs in the HR round when asked "what do you do outside work?", not in the 90 seconds that decide whether the technical conversation happens.

### Example 2 (exam level) — the mock interview, marked and diagnosed

**Task:** You observe a 20-minute mock interview. The candidate: answered "tell me about yourself" for 3 minutes 40 seconds; gave a STAR answer where the Action was "we discussed it and we fixed it"; could not answer "what's the time complexity of your solution?" and said "I don't remember, but it's fast"; asked no questions at the end. Score him on the four criteria — structure and
relevance, evidence and specificity, communication, ownership and judgement — out of 25 each, and give the three changes with the largest mark recovery.

**Solution:** *Step 1 — Score against behaviour, not impression.* **Structure and relevance: 12/25** — one answer four times the intended length (3:40 against 60–90 s), which is a structure failure, not a talking failure; the panel lost the thread and had to interrupt. **Evidence and specificity: 10/25** — the STAR answer's Action is "we discussed it and we fixed it", which contains no
action, no decision and no sequence; the Result is absent. **Communication: 16/25** — clear and audible, but no listening signal: he did not use the pause-repeat loop and answered a complexity question with a claim about speed. **Ownership and judgement: 9/25** — "we" throughout the Action, and "I don't remember, but it's fast" is a bluff in disguise: it asserts a property without
evidence. No questions at the end is a missed judgement signal, because it suggests no real interest in the role.

*Step 2 — Total:* 12 + 10 + 16 + 9 = **47/100**.

*Step 3 — Three changes, ranked by marks recovered.*

1. **Enforce a 90-second ceiling on "tell me about yourself"** (~+10). Rehearse with a timer and three keyword cards; if it runs over, cut the third evidence item, not the ending. Long answers are scored as unclear even when the content is fine.
2. **Rebuild the STAR answer so the Action is 55% of the words and the Result has a number** (~+8). "We discussed it and we fixed it" must become three sequenced "I" actions and a measurable outcome with a lesson. This single answer type appears in half of all HR rounds.
3. **Replace "I don't remember, but it's fast" with a thinking-aloud move** (~+6). Correct form: "Let me reason it out — the outer loop runs n times and the inner one over a shrinking range, so it's O(n²) time and O(1) extra space. I'd verify with a quick timing test." A wrong-but-reasoned answer scores higher than a right-but-asserted one, because the panel is buying reasoning, not
   recall.

*Step 4 — The fourth change worth mentioning but not ranking:* prepare three questions to ask at the end. It is not a marks item on this sheet, but it changes the interviewer's memory of the candidate.

## Common traps and mistakes

- **Treating the four skills as four subjects.** Revision that drills reading only will not fix an interview, because interviews are listening plus speaking. Fix: practise integrated tasks.
- **Never conceding a point.** A candidate who agrees with nothing reads as uncooperative. Fix: concede one point explicitly, then add your difference.
- **STAR with no Result.** The story stops before the outcome and reads as unfinished. Fix: always end with a number or a lesson.
- **Reciting a memorised self-introduction.** It sounds recited, and the follow-up question exposes it. Fix: memorise the spine, improvise the words.
- **"We" throughout the Action.** The panel cannot see what you did, so they cannot score it. Fix: "I" for your own actions, "we" only for genuinely shared ones.
- **Bluffing in a technical interview.** "It's fast" instead of reasoning loses the round, because the panel is buying reasoning, not recall. Fix: reason aloud, state complexity, verify with an example.
- **"Kindly do the needful" and "please look into it".** Both hand the thinking back to the reader, and both get postponed. Fix: BLUF, numbered points, a deadline.
- **Escalating before trying the direct channel.** It destroys trust faster than the original problem did. Fix: direct first, then escalate with facts, impact, options, ask.
- **Grinding full practice tests without reviewing errors.** After the second test you gain only familiarity. Fix: one test, then an error log by type.
- **A portfolio of certificates with no reflection.** Certificates prove attendance, not learning. Fix: four curated entries with evidence and a next action.

## Quick revision box

- ✅ **Integration:** listen → discuss → read → write, same content through all four stages; the test of a good integrated task is that the output cannot be produced from one input alone.
- ✅ **GD:** content ~30%, communication ~30%, teamwork ~25%, leadership ~15%. Contribute 3–4 times, build on others, bring in a quiet member, summarise near the end.
- ✅ **"Tell me about yourself":** now → how → next, 60–90 seconds, three evidence items, no self-praise adjectives, name the role.
- ✅ **STAR:** Situation 15%, Task 10%, Action 55%, Result 20%; "I" for your actions; always a number or a lesson in the Result.
- ✅ **Technical rounds:** restate, state assumptions, brute force first, think aloud, state complexity with a reason, test an edge case, say where you would look when stuck.
- ✅ **Email:** ask in the subject line, BLUF in line one, numbered points, a deadline, one topic per email. Escalate with facts → impact → options → ask.
- ✅ **CEFR and tests:** A1–C2 "can do" scale; IELTS 5.5–6.5 ≈ B2, 7.0–8.0 ≈ C1; TOEFL 72–94 ≈ B2; PTE 59–75 ≈ B2 — indicative only, and institutions set their own cut-offs per section.
- ✅ **Mock and portfolio:** define success in observable terms before practising; attack the two lowest self-ratings; one change per week, one recorded run, one rubric.

## Answers

**A1.** *Listen:* a 10-minute talk on EV adoption — output: five key points plus one question you would ask. *Discuss:* 12 minutes in groups of four on whether subsidies are the right lever — output: a group position with two supporting reasons. *Read:* a one-page article with adoption data — output: the article's thesis and one piece of evidence underlined. *Write:* a 180-word summary
of the talk plus 80 words on where the article and the talk disagree — output: a written piece that cites both sources by name. **Key step:** the written output must be impossible to produce from either input alone.

**A2.** Subject: "Status + decision needed by Thu 21 Nov — project X". First line: "I need your decision on two items by Thursday so the build can start on Friday." **Key step:** the subject carries both the ask and the date, and line one states the decision required rather than the fact that an email exists.

**A3.** "I'd push back on 'obviously' — the honest version is that *unsupervised* online exams fail, not online exams. Proctored online exams with randomised question banks solve most of the cheating problem, and cost less than hall invigilation. So the argument isn't online versus offline; it's what safeguards we attach." Techniques: (1) attack the qualifier ("obviously"), not the
person, so you disagree without hostility; (2) concede a real point (unproctored exams *do* fail) and then extend it — the "yes, and" build. **Key step:** a disagreement that ends with a constructive reformulation earns both content and teamwork marks.

**A4.** **S:** "In my first hackathon, our team of four had 24 hours and we spent the first eight building a feature the judges didn't score." **T:** "I was the one who had proposed it, so the call to drop it was mine." **A:** "I raised it at hour eight instead of hour twenty, showed the scoring rubric to the team, and proposed we cut to one working flow. Then I rewrote the plan on the
whiteboard with three milestones and took the demo script myself, because our presenter had not slept." **R:** "We shipped one flow, placed nowhere — fifth of nine — but the next team I led used the same hour-eight checkpoint, and we placed second. The lesson was that I had optimised for the interesting feature instead of the scored one." **Key step:** the Result admits the failure and
then shows the corrected behaviour later — that pairing is what makes a failure answer credible.

**A5.** *Arithmetic:* (6.5 + 6.0 + 5.5 + 6.0) / 4 = 24.0 / 4 = **6.0** now. Overall 6.5 needs an average of at least 6.25 (which rounds up to 6.5), so the sections must total **at least 25** — a gain of **1.0 band point** across the four. *Cheapest path:* Writing 5.5 → 6.0 (+0.5) and Reading 6.0 → 6.5 (+0.5) gives 25.0 → average 6.25 → **6.5 overall**. *Prioritise:* **Writing** (lowest,
and half a band is very reachable from task-response work) and **Reading** (already close; time management alone often moves it half a band). Leave Listening and Speaking alone — they are at or above target and each extra half band there costs more effort than the same half band in Writing. **Key step:** check any per-section floor too — a 6.5 overall with Writing exactly 6.0 meets "no
band below 6.0" only just, so confirm the wording.

**A6.** "Subject: Blocked — staging API returning 500 since Mon 10:00 (release Fri 22) *(facts, in the subject)*. Hi Sana — the staging API has returned 500 on every call since Monday 10:00; request IDs and timestamps attached *(facts)*. I messaged the team channel on Monday and again Wednesday with no reply *(facts — the prior-attempt record)*. Our payments module cannot be
integration-tested and the release is Friday *(impact)*. Two options: a new key today and we retest tomorrow, or the sandbox key with reduced scope to unblock now *(options)*; I recommend the first. Can you confirm by Thursday 10:00? *(ask)*" **Key step:** facts before any statement of feeling, prior attempts documented rather than described as being ignored, and the email ends with a
decision request and a time — which makes it an escalation, not a complaint.
